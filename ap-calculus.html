TEMEL KAVRAMLAR

AP Calculus Theoretical Foundations & Concept Mastery

Module 1: Limits, Continuity & The Foundational Existence Theorems Unit 1 & 2 • Rigor

Formal Definition of Continuity: A function $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = c$ if and only if three conditions are satisfied:
1. f(c) is defined (c is in the domain of f)
2. \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) \text{ exists (i.e., }\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L\text{)}\)
3. \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)\)

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT): If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, and $u$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one number $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = u$. Application: To prove a root exists ($f(c) = 0$), show that $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs and explicitly state that $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.
College Board Rubric Trap: Never write "by IVT there is a root" without first writing: "Because $f(x)$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$...". Failing to state the continuity hypothesis forfeits the justification point automatically!
Module 2: Differential Calculus, The Mean Value Theorem & Optimization Unit 3, 4 & 5 • Core Mechanics

The Derivative as an Instantaneous Rate of Change: The derivative is defined rigorously as the limit of the difference quotient:
\(f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} [f(x + h) - f(x)] / h = \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - f(a)] / (x - a)\)
Theorem: Differentiability implies continuity ($f \text{ differentiable at } c \implies f \text{ continuous at } c$). The converse is FALSE (e.g., $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$ has a sharp corner).

Mean Value Theorem (MVT): If $f(x)$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that:
f'(c) = [f(b) - f(a)] / (b - a)
Geometric Interpretation: The instantaneous rate of change (tangent slope) equals the average rate of change (secant slope) at least once within the interval.
Extreme Value Theorem (EVT) & The Candidates Test: A continuous function on a closed interval $[a, b]$ must attain an absolute maximum and minimum. To find absolute extrema:
1. Find all critical points in $(a, b)$ where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'(x)$ is undefined.
2. Evaluate $f(x)$ at all critical points AND at the endpoints $a$ and $b$.
3. The largest value is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.
Module 3: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) & Accumulation Unit 6 & 8 • Integral Architecture

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus bridges the two central branches of mathematical analysis:

FTC Part 1 (Derivative of an Accumulation Function): If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then $g(x) = \int_a^x f(t) dt$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and:
g'(x) = d/dx [ ∫_a^x f(t) dt ] = f(x)
Chain Rule Extension: \(d/dx [ \int _a^{u(x)} f(t) dt ] = f(u(x)) \cdot u'(x)\)

FTC Part 2 (Evaluation Theorem & Net Change):
∫_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a) where F'(x) = f(x)
Final Position = Initial Position + Displacement: s(b) = s(a) + ∫_a^b v(t) dt

Displacement vs. Total Distance: Displacement is simply $\int_a^b v(t) dt$. Total distance traveled accounts for reversals and requires the absolute value integral: $\int_a^b |v(t)| dt$. Always find the roots of $v(t) = 0$ and integrate piecewise when calculating by hand!
Module 4: Advanced Series, Taylor Expansions & Error Bounds (BC Scope) Unit 10 • BC Deep-Dive

Taylor Polynomial Centered at $x = c$:
\(P_n(x) = f(c) + f'(c)(x - c) + [f''(c)/2!](x - c)^2 + ... + [f^{(n)}(c)/n!](x - c)^n\)
For Maclaurin series ($c = 0$), memorize the 4 canonical power series:
• $e^x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^n}{n!} = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^3}{3!} + ... \quad (\forall x \in \mathbb{R})$
• $\sin(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n \frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!} = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!} - ... \quad (\forall x \in \mathbb{R})$
• $\cos(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n \frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - ... \quad (\forall x \in \mathbb{R})$
• $\frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + ... \quad (|x| < 1)$

Lagrange Error Bound (Taylor's Remainder Theorem): If $|f^{(n+1)}(t)| \le M$ for all $t$ between $c$ and $x$, then the error in approximating $f(x)$ with $P_n(x)$ is bounded by:
\(|R_n(x)| = |f(x) - P_n(x)| \le [M / (n + 1)!] \cdot |x - c|^{n + 1}\)
UNIT 1 · KONU ANLATIMI

Unit 1: Limits and Continuity

1.1 Intuitive Concept of Limits & The Formal (ε - δ) Definition Topic 1.1

Intuitive Definition: We write \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\) if as $x$ approaches $c$ from both sides (left and right), $f(x)$ becomes arbitrarily close to the finite real number $L$. Notice that $x \ne c$; limits describe behavior near a point, completely independent of the value $f(c)$.

Formal Cauchy-Weierstrass (ε - δ) Definition:
Let $f$ be a function defined on an open interval containing $c$, except possibly at $c$ itself. We say that \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\) if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a corresponding $\delta > 0$ such that:
0 < |x - c| < δ ⟹ |f(x) - L| < ε.
Proof Mechanism: Given a challenge error tolerance $\varepsilon$ around the target limit $L$, we must construct an input radius $\delta$ around $c$ such that all $x$ within the punctured interval $(c - \delta, c) \cup (c, c + \delta)$ map inside $(L - \varepsilon, L + \varepsilon)$.

Existence Criterion: The two-sided limit exists if and only if both one-sided limits exist and are equal:
\(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L\).

1.2 Algebraic Limit Laws, Indeterminate Forms (0/0) & Analytical Evaluation Topic 1.2

When evaluating $\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$, always begin with Direct Substitution:
• If $f(c) = L$ (a real number) and $f$ is continuous, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$.
• If substitution yields $\frac{k}{0}$ ($k \ne 0$), the limit is infinite ($\pm\infty$) or does not exist (Vertical Asymptote).
• If substitution yields $\frac{0}{0}$, this is an Indeterminate Form! The limit exists and can be resolved through algebraic cancellation or trigonometric identities.

Three Canonical Algebraic Techniques for 0/0:
1. Factoring & Cancellation: $\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = \lim_{x \to 3} (x+3) = 6$.
2. Conjugate Rationalization: For radicals, multiply by $\frac{\sqrt{A} + B}{\sqrt{A} + B}$ to exploit the difference of squares: $(\sqrt{A} - B)(\sqrt{A} + B) = A - B^2$.
3. Complex Fractions (LCD Method): Multiply numerator and denominator by the common denominator to clear embedded fractions.
Special Trigonometric Limits (Must Memorize):
\(\lim_{x \to 0} [sin(x) / x] = 1\) and \(\lim_{x \to 0} [(1 - cos(x)) / x] = 0\).
Generalized: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(ax)}{bx} = \frac{a}{b}$ and $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan(ax)}{\sin(bx)} = \frac{a}{b}$.
1.3 The Squeeze (Sandwich) Theorem & Bounded Functions Topic 1.3

Theorem Statement: If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ in an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$), and if:
\(\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L\),
then \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L\).

Canonical AP Proof Model: Show that $\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin(1/x) = 0$.
1. Bounding inequality: $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$ for all $x \ne 0$.
2. Multiply across by $x^2 \ge 0$: $-x^2 \le x^2 \sin(1/x) \le x^2$.
3. Evaluate bounds: $\lim_{x \to 0} (-x^2) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to 0} (x^2) = 0$.
4. Conclusion: By the Squeeze Theorem, $\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin(1/x) = 0$.
1.4 & 1.5 Continuity at a Point, Interval Continuity & Classification of Discontinuities Topic 1.4 & 1.5

The 3-Condition Continuity Test (College Board Rubric Requirement):
A function $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = c$ if and only if ALL THREE conditions hold:
1. f(c) is defined (c is in the domain of f).
2. \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) \text{ exists (i.e., }\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x)\text{)}\).
3. \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)\).

Taxonomy of Discontinuities:
• Removable Discontinuity (Hole): $\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ exists, but $f(c)$ is undefined or $\lim \ne f(c)$. Can be repaired by redefining $f(c) = L$.
• Jump Discontinuity: $\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) \ne \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x)$ (both one-sided limits are finite but unequal). Common in piecewise and $|x|/x$ functions.
• Infinite Discontinuity (Essential): At least one one-sided limit equals $\pm\infty$ (Vertical Asymptote). Occurs when denominator is zero and cannot be canceled.
1.6 The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) & Root-Finding Existence Topic 1.6

Theorem Statement: If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, and $N$ is any value strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ (where $f(a) \ne f(b)$), then there exists at least one number $c \in (a, b)$ such that:
f(c) = N.

College Board Point-Scoring Formula for IVT:
To earn all rubric points on an IVT FRQ, write these 3 exact sentences:
1. "Since $f(x)$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$..." (1 pt)
2. "Since $f(a) = \text{[val]} < 0 < \text{[val]} = f(b)$..." (1 pt)
3. "By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = 0$." (1 pt)
1.7 Limits at Infinity (x → ±∞) & Horizontal / Vertical Asymptotes Topic 1.7

Horizontal Asymptotes (HA): The line $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote of $y = f(x)$ if:
\(\lim_{x \to \infty } f(x) = L \quad\text{or}\quad \lim_{x \to -\infty } f(x) = L\).
A function can have at most TWO horizontal asymptotes (one as $x \to +\infty$ and another as $x \to -\infty$).

The Radical Trap as $x \to -\infty$:
For $x < 0$, $\sqrt{x^2} = |x| = -x$.
Therefore, $\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}}{3x - 2} = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{|x|\sqrt{4 + 1/x^2}}{x(3 - 2/x)} = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{-x\sqrt{4 + 1/x^2}}{x(3 - 2/x)} = -\frac{2}{3}$! (Not $+2/3$!)

Akılda kal

  • İki taraflı limit, ancak sol ve sağ limitler var ve birbirine eşitse vardır; limit f(c) değerinden bağımsızdır.
  • Önce doğrudan yerine koy: 0/0 belirsizdir (çarpanlara ayır, eşlenikle çarp, payda eşitle); k/0 (k ≠ 0) dikey asimptot işaretidir.
  • x → 0 (radyan): sin x / x → 1 ve (1 − cos x)/x → 0; sıkıştırma teoremi sınırlı çarpanlarda işe yarar.
  • Süreklilik üç koşuldur: f(c) tanımlı, lim f(x) var, lim f(x) = f(c). Süreksizlik türleri: kaldırılabilir, sıçrama, sonsuz.
  • IVT yalnız kapalı aralıkta sürekli fonksiyonda çalışır: f(a) ile f(b) arasındaki her değer en az bir kez alınır.

Unit 2: Differentiation — Definition and Fundamental Derivative Rules

2.1 & 2.2 The Limit Definition of the Derivative & Geometric Tangent Lines Topic 2.1 & 2.2

The Derivative as a Function: The derivative of a function $f(x)$ with respect to $x$ is defined as the limit of the difference quotient as the interval width $h \to 0$:
\(f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{dy}{dx}\)
Alternative Definition at a Fixed Point $x = c$:
\(f'(c) = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}\)

College Board Recognition Skill (Reverse Limit Identification):
Many AP multiple-choice questions do not ask you to compute a limit by algebraic manipulation; instead, they give you a limit that represents $f'(c)$ in disguise!
Example: $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos(\frac{\pi}{3} + h) - \frac{1}{2}}{h}$ represents $\frac{d}{dx}[\cos x]$ evaluated at $x = \frac{\pi}{3}$. Since $f'(x) = -\sin x$, the limit equals $-\sin(\frac{\pi}{3}) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$!

Tangent and Normal Line Equations:
• Tangent Line at $(c, f(c))$: $y - f(c) = f'(c)(x - c)$
• Normal Line (Perpendicular) at $(c, f(c))$: $y - f(c) = -\frac{1}{f'(c)}(x - c) \quad (f'(c) \ne 0)$.

2.3 Differentiability vs. Continuity & Criteria for Non-Differentiability Topic 2.3

The Golden Theorem: If a function $f$ is differentiable at $x = c$, then $f$ is continuous at $x = c$.

Formal Proof of Theorem (Differentiability ⟹ Continuity):
We must prove that $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, which is equivalent to proving $\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) - f(c)] = 0$.
1. For all $x \ne c$, write the identity: $f(x) - f(c) = \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \cdot (x - c)$.
2. Take limits as $x \to c$ on both sides using the Product Law of Limits:
$$\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) - f(c)] = \lim_{x \to c} \left[\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}\right] \cdot \lim_{x \to c} (x - c) = f'(c) \cdot 0 = 0$$.
3. Since $\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) - f(c)] = 0$, it follows that $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$. Hence $f$ is continuous at $c$. $\blacksquare$
CRITICAL WARNING: The Converse is FALSE! (Continuity ⇏ Differentiability):
A function can be perfectly continuous at a point but fail to be differentiable. The 4 causes of non-differentiability are:
1. Sharp Corner (Node): Left-hand derivative $\ne$ Right-hand derivative (e.g., $f(x) = |x|$ at $x=0$, where $f'_-(0) = -1$ and $f'_+(0) = +1$).
2. Cusp: One-sided derivative approaches $+\infty$ while the other approaches $-\infty$ (e.g., $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ at $x = 0$).
3. Vertical Tangent: Both one-sided derivatives approach $+\infty$ or both approach $-\infty$ (e.g., $f(x) = x^{1/3}$ at $x = 0$).
4. Discontinuity: If $f$ is not continuous at $c$, it cannot be differentiable at $c$.
2.4 & 2.5 Elementary Rules: Power Rule, Exponentials & Logarithms Topic 2.4 & 2.5

The General Power Rule: For any real exponent $n \in \mathbb{R}$:
\(\frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n \cdot x^{n - 1}\)
Radicals & Negative Exponents: Always rewrite roots and denominators into rational powers before taking derivatives:
• $\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{x^3}\right] = \frac{d}{dx}[x^{-3}] = -3x^{-4} = -\frac{3}{x^4}$
• $\frac{d}{dx}[\sqrt[3]{x^2}] = \frac{d}{dx}[x^{2/3}] = \frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x}}$

Exponential and Logarithmic Derivatives:
• Natural Exponential: $\frac{d}{dx}[e^x] = e^x$
• General Base Exponential ($a > 0$): $\frac{d}{dx}[a^x] = a^x \cdot \ln(a)$
• Natural Logarithm: $\frac{d}{dx}[\ln(x)] = \frac{1}{x} \quad (x > 0)$
• General Base Logarithm: $\frac{d}{dx}[\log_a(x)] = \frac{1}{x \ln(a)}$
Constant Base vs. Variable Base Trap:
Do NOT confuse $x^n$ (variable base, constant power $\implies$ Power Rule) with $a^x$ (constant base, variable power $\implies$ Exponential Rule):
• $\frac{d}{dx}[x^e] = e x^{e-1}$ (Power Rule)
• $\frac{d}{dx}[e^x] = e^x$ (Exponential Rule)
• $\frac{d}{dx}[e^\pi] = 0$ (Constant Rule! $e^\pi$ has no variable $x$!)
2.6 Derivatives of the Six Trigonometric Functions Topic 2.6

The derivatives of the six fundamental trigonometric functions must be memorized instantaneously:

\(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin(x)] = \cos(x)\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}[\cos(x)] = -\sin(x)\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}[\tan(x)] = \sec^2(x)\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}[\csc(x)] = -\csc(x)\cot(x)\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}[\sec(x)] = \sec(x)\tan(x)\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}[\cot(x)] = -\csc^2(x)\)
Mnemonic for Signs: Notice that every trigonometric function starting with "co-" ($\cos$, $\csc$, $\cot$) has a negative derivative! All other three functions ($\sin$, $\tan$, $\sec$) have positive derivatives.
2.7 The Product Rule & The Quotient Rule (Rigor & Pitfalls) Topic 2.7

The Product Rule: The derivative of a product is NOT the product of derivatives!
\(\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \cdot g(x)] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)\)

Proof of Product Rule:
Let $P(x) = f(x)g(x)$. Using the limit definition:
$$P'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}$$
Add and subtract the cross-term $f(x+h)g(x)$ in the numerator:
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x+h)g(x) + f(x+h)g(x) - f(x)g(x)}{h}$$
$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ f(x+h) \cdot \frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h} + g(x) \cdot \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \right]$$
Since $f$ is differentiable, it is continuous, so $\lim_{h \to 0} f(x+h) = f(x)$. Thus:
$$P'(x) = f(x)g'(x) + g(x)f'(x). \quad \blacksquare$$

The Quotient Rule: For $g(x) \ne 0$:
\(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2} = \frac{\text{Low } d(\text{High}) - \text{High } d(\text{Low})}{\text{Low}^2}\)

Deadly AP Sign Reversal Trap:
Because subtraction is not commutative, the numerator of the Quotient Rule MUST be $\mathbf{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}$ (Low $d$-High minus High $d$-Low). Swapping the terms to $f(x)g'(x) - f'(x)g(x)$ multiplies your answer by $-1$, which is always included as a deliberate distractor by College Board!

Akılda kal

  • f′(a) = lim(h→0) [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h: kesenlerin eğimi teğetin eğimine yaklaşır.
  • Türevlenebilir ⇒ sürekli; tersi doğru değildir (köşe, sivri uç, dikey teğet, süreksizlik).
  • Temel kurallar: (xⁿ)′ = nxⁿ⁻¹, (eˣ)′ = eˣ, (ln x)′ = 1/x, (sin x)′ = cos x, (cos x)′ = −sin x, (tan x)′ = sec²x.
  • Product: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′. Quotient: (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′)/g² — paydaki sıra önemlidir.

Unit 3: Composite, Implicit, and Inverse Functions

Beyond elementary rules, calculus models interdependent systems via the Chain Rule, implicit relations, and inverse functions. Here are the core theorems, derivations, and exam-level techniques required for College Board 5/5 mastery:

3.1 The Chain Rule for Composite Functions Topic 3.1

The Chain Rule: If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composite function $F(x) = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x$:
\(F'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \quad \text{or in Leibniz notation:} \quad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}\)

Decomposition of Nested Functions:
When differentiating multi-layered composite functions, work systematically from the outside in ("peeling the onion"):
For $y = f(g(h(x)))$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(h(x))) \cdot g'(h(x)) \cdot h'(x)$.
Example: $\frac{d}{dx}[\sin^4(3x^2)] = 4\sin^3(3x^2) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[\sin(3x^2)] = 4\sin^3(3x^2) \cdot \cos(3x^2) \cdot (6x) = 24x\sin^3(3x^2)\cos(3x^2)$.
The "Forgotten Inner Derivative" Trap: The most frequent student error on AP exam free-response and multiple-choice questions is forgetting to multiply by the derivative of the inside function $g'(x)$. Always ask yourself: "Did I multiply by the derivative of the inside?"
3.2 Implicit Differentiation & Higher-Order Derivatives Topic 3.2

When an equation relates $x$ and $y$ implicitly (cannot be easily solved for $y=f(x)$), differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a differentiable function of $x$ (applying the Chain Rule $\frac{d}{dx}[y^n] = n y^{n-1} \frac{dy}{dx}$):

Algorithmic Protocol for Implicit Differentiation:
1. Differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to $x$, applying the product, quotient, and chain rules wherever $y$ appears.
2. Collect all terms containing $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (or $y'$) on one side and all other terms on the opposite side.
3. Factor out $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and solve for it algebraically: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\text{terms without } y'}{\text{coefficients of } y'}$.
4. For Second Derivative $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$: Differentiate $\frac{dy}{dx}$ with respect to $x$ (often using the Quotient Rule), then substitute the expression for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ back into the result to obtain $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ purely in terms of $x$ and $y$.
3.3 & 3.4 Derivatives of Inverse Functions & Inverse Trigonometric Functions Topic 3.3 & 3.4

Derivative of an Inverse Function: If $f$ is strictly monotonic and differentiable with inverse $g = f^{-1}$, and $f(b) = a$ (so $g(a) = b$), then:
\(g'(a) = (f^{-1})'(a) = \frac{1}{f'(g(a))} = \frac{1}{f'(b)} \quad \text{provided } f'(b) \ne 0\)
Geometric Insight: Tangent lines of inverse functions across $y = x$ have reciprocal slopes.

Derivatives of Inverse Trigonometric Functions:
• \frac{d}{dx}[\arcsin(x)] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \quad |x| < 1
• \frac{d}{dx}[\arccos(x)] = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \quad |x| < 1
• \(\frac{d}{dx}[\arctan(x)] = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}, \quad x \in \mathbb{R}\)
• \(\frac{d}{dx}[\text{arcsec}(x)] = \frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^2 - 1}}, \quad |x| > 1\)
Chain Rule with Inverse Trig: Never forget to chain out: $\frac{d}{dx}[\arctan(u)] = \frac{1}{1 + u^2} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$. For example: $\frac{d}{dx}[\arctan(3x)] = \frac{3}{1 + 9x^2}$.

Akılda kal

  • Chain rule: d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x))·g′(x) — dış fonksiyonun türevi, iç fonksiyon olduğu gibi kalır.
  • Implicit differentiation: iki tarafın x'e göre türevini al; y içeren her terim dy/dx ile çarpılır.
  • Ters fonksiyon: f(a) = b ise (f⁻¹)′(b) = 1 / f′(a).
  • (arcsin x)′ = 1/√(1 − x²), (arctan x)′ = 1/(1 + x²).

Unit 4: Contextual Applications of Differentiation — Dynamic Modeling & Rates

4.1 Interpreting Meaning of the Derivative & Units in Applied Contexts Topic 4.1

The Fundamental Interpretation Template for AP FRQs:
When asked to "explain the meaning of $f'(c) = k$ in the context of the problem including units", your answer must strictly contain three components:

The 3 Mandatory Scoring Elements:
1. Specific Moment in Time ($t = c$): Always anchor to the exact input value with units (e.g., "At time $t = 5$ minutes...").
2. Contextual Quantity & Direction of Change: If $k > 0$, state the quantity is increasing; if $k < 0$, state the quantity is decreasing (never say "decreasing at a rate of $-k$").
3. Rate Value with Ratio Units: $\text{Units of } f'(t) = \frac{\text{units of } f}{\text{units of } t}$ (e.g., "$1.8^\circ\text{C}$ per minute").
College Board Grading Penalty: Omitting the units or writing "The temperature changes by $-1.8$ over 5 minutes" awards ZERO points. The derivative is an instantaneous rate of change, not an average or cumulative change!
4.2 Straight-Line Motion: Position, Velocity, Acceleration & Speed Dynamics Topic 4.2

For a particle moving along a straight line with position $x(t)$ (or $s(t)$):

Kinematic Derivative Chain:
• Velocity: $v(t) = x'(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}$. ($v > 0 \implies$ moving right/up; $v < 0 \implies$ moving left/down).
• Acceleration: $a(t) = v'(t) = x''(t) = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$.
• Particle Changes Direction: Requires $v(t) = 0$ AND $v(t)$ must change sign across that critical moment!
• Speed: The absolute magnitude of velocity: $\text{Speed} = |v(t)|$.
The #1 Student Misconception: "Speeding Up" vs. "Slowing Down":
Never look at acceleration alone! Speed is the absolute magnitude of velocity:
• A particle is speeding up if and only if velocity and acceleration have the SAME SIGN ($v(t) \cdot a(t) > 0$).
• A particle is slowing down if and only if velocity and acceleration have OPPOSITE SIGNS ($v(t) \cdot a(t) < 0$).
Example: If $v(t) = -6\text{ m/s}$ and $a(t) = -3\text{ m/s}^2$, the particle is moving in the negative direction and SPEEDING UP ($|-6| \to |-9| = 9\text{ m/s}$)!
4.4 & 4.5 Related Rates: The 5-Step Algorithmic Protocol Topic 4.4 & 4.5

In related rates problems, all geometric and physical quantities are implicit functions of time $t$. Differentiating with respect to time requires systematic application of the Chain Rule ($\frac{d}{dt}$):

The 5-Step AP Calculus Master Protocol:
1. Diagram & Variable Tagging: Draw a sketch. Label quantities that change with variables ($x, y, r, h, \theta$); label quantities that remain strictly constant throughout the motion with numbers (e.g., fixed ladder length $= 10\text{ ft}$).
2. Identify Given & Target Rates: Explicitly write down $\frac{d(\cdot)}{dt}$ given and $\frac{d(\cdot)}{dt}$ sought, noting signs ($\text{shrinking/decreasing} \implies \text{negative}$).
3. Geometric Equation: Write an equation relating the variables (Pythagorean $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$, Conical Volume $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$, Trig $\tan\theta = y/x$). If a variable is unneeded and its rate is unknown, use geometry (e.g., similar triangles $\frac{r}{h} = \frac{R}{H} \implies r = \frac{R}{H}h$) to eliminate it BEFORE differentiating!
4. Implicit Time Derivative: Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$ using the Chain Rule ($\frac{d}{dt}$).
5. Instantaneous Substitution: Substitute numerical values valid only at that specific instant and solve algebraically for the target rate.
The Fatal Premature Substitution Trap: Never substitute instantaneous values (such as $h = 3\text{ m}$ or $x = 6\text{ ft}$) before taking the derivative! If you substitute numbers into variables before differentiating, their derivative becomes $0$, destroying the entire problem!
4.6 Local Linearity, Tangent Line Approximation & Concavity Bounds Topic 4.6

Local Linearity: Differentiable curves look locally like straight lines when zoomed in near a point of tangency $(a, f(a))$. The tangent line approximation (linearization) is: $$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$$

Determining Overestimate vs. Underestimate Using Second Derivatives ($f''(x)$):
• Concave Down ($f''(x) < 0$ on the interval): The graph bends below its tangent line. The tangent line lies strictly ABOVE the curve $\implies L(x)$ is an OVERESTIMATE ($L(x) > f(x)$).
• Concave Up ($f''(x) > 0$ on the interval): The graph bends above its tangent line. The tangent line lies strictly BELOW the curve $\implies L(x)$ is an UNDERESTIMATE ($L(x) < f(x)$).
4.7 L'Hôpital's Rule for Indeterminate Forms (0/0 & ±∞/±∞) Topic 4.7

L'Hôpital's Rule Theorem: Suppose $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$) and $g'(x) \ne 0$ near $c$. If $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = 0$ (or both $\pm\infty$), then: $$\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

College Board Mandatory Communication Standard for 2025/2026:
To earn full points on free-response questions, you must:
1. Separately verify and write: $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = 0$.
2. DO NOT write $\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{0}{0}$! "$\frac{0}{0}$" is not a number; equating a limit to $\frac{0}{0}$ with an equals sign constitutes a mathematical error and results in the forfeiture of the point.
3. Explicitly declare that L'Hôpital's Rule applies, and differentiate the numerator and denominator independently (do NOT use the Quotient Rule!).

Akılda kal

  • Türevin birimi: çıktı birimi / girdi birimi; yorumda zamanı, miktarı ve birimi bağlamıyla yaz.
  • Doğrusal hareket: v = x′, a = v′; v ve a aynı işaretliyse speed artar, zıtsa azalır.
  • Related rates: çiz, değişkenleri tanımla, ilişki denklemini kur, t'ye göre türev al, ancak sonra sayıları yerleştir.
  • Lineer yaklaşım L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a); f içbükey yukarıysa (f″ > 0) teğet alt tahmin verir.
  • L'Hôpital yalnız 0/0 veya ∞/∞ için: pay ve paydanın türevleri ayrı ayrı alınır (bölüm kuralı değil).

Unit 5: Analytical Applications of Differentiation — Optimization & Graph Analysis

5.1 & 5.2 The Mean Value Theorem (MVT) & The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT) Topic 5.1 & 5.2

Both theorems are existence theorems: they guarantee that a certain value exists, but do not provide an explicit formula to find it.

The Mean Value Theorem (MVT):
If a function $f$ satisfies two mandatory preconditions:
1. $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, and
2. $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$,
then there exists at least one number $c \in (a, b)$ such that: $$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$ Geometric Interpretation: The instantaneous rate of change (tangent line slope) at $c$ equals the average rate of change (secant line slope) across $[a, b]$.
• Rolle's Theorem: If additionally $f(a) = f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.
DEADLY AP FRQ TRAP — MVT Precondition Verification:
On College Board FRQs, you receive ZERO points for applying MVT if you do not explicitly state: "Since $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, by the Mean Value Theorem...". If $f$ has a cusp, vertical tangent, or jump discontinuity anywhere on the interval, MVT CANNOT BE APPLIED!
The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT) & The Candidates Test:
If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ MUST attain both an absolute (global) maximum and an absolute (global) minimum on $[a, b]$.
The 3-Step Candidates Test for Absolute Extrema:
1. Find all critical points $c \in (a, b)$ where $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ is undefined.
2. Evaluate $f(x)$ at all critical points $c$ within the interval.
3. Evaluate $f(x)$ at the endpoints $x = a$ and $x = b$. The largest resulting value is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum!
5.3 & 5.4 Determining Intervals of Increase/Decrease & The First Derivative Test Topic 5.3 & 5.4

Monotonicity Criteria:
• If $f'(x) > 0$ on an open interval, then $f$ is strictly increasing on that interval.
• If $f'(x) < 0$ on an open interval, then $f$ is strictly decreasing on that interval.
• If $f'(x) = 0$ on an interval, then $f$ is constant on that interval.

The First Derivative Test for Local (Relative) Extrema:
Let $c$ be a critical point of a continuous function $f$:
1. Local Maximum: If $f'(x)$ changes from positive to negative at $c$, then $f$ has a relative maximum at $c$.
2. Local Minimum: If $f'(x)$ changes from negative to positive at $c$, then $f$ has a relative minimum at $c$.
3. No Extremum: If $f'(x)$ does not change sign at $c$ (e.g., positive on both sides, like $f(x) = x^3$ at $x = 0$), then $c$ is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.
AP Justification Rule: Never write "because the graph goes up and down"! Always justify relative extrema mathematically: "$f$ has a relative maximum at $x = c$ because $f'(x)$ changes sign from positive to negative at $x = c$."
5.5, 5.6 & 5.7 Concavity, Points of Inflection & The Second Derivative Test Topic 5.5 - 5.7

Concavity and the Second Derivative:
• If $f''(x) > 0$ on an interval (or equivalently, $f'(x)$ is increasing), the graph of $f$ is concave up (holds water; tangent lines lie BELOW the curve).
• If $f''(x) < 0$ on an interval (or equivalently, $f'(x)$ is decreasing), the graph of $f$ is concave down (spills water; tangent lines lie ABOVE the curve).

Point of Inflection (POI) Strict Definition:
A point $(c, f(c))$ is a point of inflection if and only if:
1. $f$ is continuous at $c$, AND
2. The graph of $f$ changes concavity at $c$ (i.e., $f''(x)$ changes sign from positive to negative OR negative to positive).
Critical Trap: Having $f''(c) = 0$ is a necessary candidate condition, but NOT sufficient! For example, for $f(x) = x^4$, $f''(0) = 0$, but $f''(x) = 12x^2 \ge 0$ for all $x$, so there is NO sign change and NO inflection point at $x = 0$!
The Second Derivative Test for Relative Extrema:
Suppose $f''(x)$ is continuous near $c$ and $f'(c) = 0$:
• If $f''(c) < 0$, then $f$ is concave down at $c \implies f$ has a relative maximum at $c$.
• If $f''(c) > 0$, then $f$ is concave up at $c \implies f$ has a relative minimum at $c$.
• If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is INCONCLUSIVE! You must revert to the First Derivative Test.
5.8 & 5.9 Graphical Relationships Between $f$, $f'$, and $f''$ Topic 5.8 & 5.9

The most heavily tested skill on AP Calculus exams is extracting behavior of $f$ when given the graph of $f'$:

Master Translation Table (Given the Graph of $f'$):
• Where $f'$ is above the x-axis ($f' > 0$) $\iff f$ is increasing.
• Where $f'$ is below the x-axis ($f' < 0$) $\iff f$ is decreasing.
• Where $f'$ crosses the x-axis from above to below $\iff f$ has a local maximum.
• Where $f'$ crosses the x-axis from below to above $\iff f$ has a local minimum.
• Where $f'$ is increasing (slope of $f' > 0$) $\iff f'' > 0 \iff f$ is concave up.
• Where $f'$ is decreasing (slope of $f' < 0$) $\iff f'' < 0 \iff f$ is concave down.
• Where $f'$ has a horizontal tangent / relative peak or valley $\iff f'$ changes between increasing and decreasing $\iff f$ has a point of inflection.
5.10 & 5.11 Applied Optimization Protocols (The 5-Step Engineering Algorithm) Topic 5.10 & 5.11

Optimization problems require finding the absolute maximum or minimum value of a modeled quantity subject to physical geometric or economic constraints:

The 5-Step Optimization Protocol:
1. Variables & Primary Equation: Identify the quantity to be maximized/minimized (e.g., Volume $V$, Area $A$, Cost $C$).
2. Secondary Constraint Equation: Relate the variables using given restrictions (e.g., Surface area $= 100\text{ cm}^2$, Perimeter $= 40\text{ m}$) and substitute to express the primary equation in terms of a single variable ($f(x)$).
3. Feasible Domain: Determine physical boundaries on the variable (e.g., $0 < x < L/2$).
4. Differentiation & Critical Points: Compute $f'(x)$, set $f'(x) = 0$, and solve for critical values.
5. Absolute Extrema Justification: On a closed interval, use the Candidates Test. On an open interval with only ONE critical point, use the First or Second Derivative Test to prove it is an absolute extremum: "Since $f'(c) = 0$ and $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < c$ and $f'(x) < 0$ for $x > c$, $x = c$ is the unique absolute maximum."

Akılda kal

  • MVT: [a, b]'de sürekli ve (a, b)'de türevlenebilir ise f′(c) = [f(b) − f(a)]/(b − a) olan bir c vardır.
  • EVT: kapalı aralıkta sürekli f mutlak max ve min alır; adaylar kritik noktalar ve uç noktalardır.
  • İlk türev testi: f′ işareti + → − değişirse yerel max, − → + ise yerel min.
  • Dönüm noktası için f″ işaret değiştirmelidir; yalnız f″(c) = 0 yetmez.
  • İkinci türev testi: f′(c) = 0 ve f″(c) < 0 ⇒ yerel max; f″(c) > 0 ⇒ yerel min; f″(c) = 0 ⇒ sonuçsuz.

UNIT 6 · KONU ANLATIMI

AP Calculus AB/BC — Unit 6: Integration and Accumulation of Change

Differential calculus measures how fast a quantity changes; integral calculus reconstructs how much has changed. This unit moves from Riemann-sum approximations to the definite integral, the two parts of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, accumulation functions, and the core antidifferentiation techniques. Unit 6 carries 15–20% of the exam score on both AP Calculus AB and BC (College Board, AP Students course pages). Sections marked BC ONLY are tested only on AP Calculus BC.

6.1 & 6.2 Accumulation of Change & Riemann Sum Approximations Topic 6.1 & 6.2

Core idea: If $r(t)$ is a rate of change, then the area between the graph of $r$ and the $t$-axis represents the accumulated change in the underlying quantity. Units always multiply: $\text{(liters/minute)} \times \text{(minutes)} = \text{liters}$. Area below the axis counts as negative accumulation.

The Four Approximation Methods on $[a, b]$ with partition points $x_0 \lt x_1 \lt \dots \lt x_n$ and widths $\Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1}$:
• Left Riemann sum: $L = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i-1})\,\Delta x_i$
• Right Riemann sum: $R = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x_i$
• Midpoint Riemann sum: $M = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f\!\left(\frac{x_{i-1} + x_i}{2}\right)\Delta x_i$
• Trapezoidal sum: $T = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{f(x_{i-1}) + f(x_i)}{2}\,\Delta x_i = \frac{L + R}{2}$

Worked table example (unequal subintervals):

$t$ (min) 0 3 4 8
$v(t)$ (m/min) 10 14 13 9

Right Riemann sum for $\int_0^8 v(t)\,dt$: $3(14) + 1(13) + 4(9) = 42 + 13 + 36 = 91$ meters. Trapezoidal sum: $3\cdot\frac{10 + 14}{2} + 1\cdot\frac{14 + 13}{2} + 4\cdot\frac{13 + 9}{2} = 36 + 13.5 + 44 = 93.5$ meters.

Over- vs. Underestimate Decision Table (for $f$ on the whole interval):
• $f$ increasing ⟹ $L$ underestimates, $R$ overestimates.
• $f$ decreasing ⟹ $L$ overestimates, $R$ underestimates.
• $f$ concave up ⟹ $T$ overestimates, $M$ underestimates.
• $f$ concave down ⟹ $T$ underestimates, $M$ overestimates.
Common Trap — Assuming Equal Widths: AP tables almost always use unequal subintervals. Never use $\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$ on a table; compute every $\Delta x_i$ separately. Also, a table alone does not tell you whether $f$ is increasing or concave up — you may only classify an estimate if the problem states monotonicity or concavity.
6.3 Riemann Sums, Summation Notation & the Definite Integral Topic 6.3

Definition: If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, $\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$ and $x_k = a + k\,\Delta x$, then $$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k)\,\Delta x.$$

Reverse-Engineering Protocol (Sum ⟶ Integral):
1. Identify $\Delta x$ (the factor outside $f$, e.g. $\frac{3}{n}$) ⟹ $b - a = 3$.
2. Identify $x_k = a + k\Delta x$ inside $f$ (e.g. $1 + \frac{3k}{n}$) ⟹ $a = 1$, $b = 4$.
3. Read off $f$: $\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{3}{n}\sqrt{1 + \frac{3k}{n}} = \int_1^4 \sqrt{x}\,dx$ (equivalently $\int_0^3 \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx$).
Common Trap: Several integrals can represent the same limit (shifted variables). Before choosing an answer, check that the interval length matches $\Delta x$ and that the integrand evaluated at the left endpoint matches the $k = 0$ term.
6.4 & 6.5 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 1) & Accumulation Functions Topic 6.4 & 6.5

FTC Part 1: If $f$ is continuous on an interval containing $a$, then the accumulation function $g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is differentiable and $g'(x) = f(x)$.

Chain Rule Versions (most tested form):
$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x) \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\int_{v(x)}^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x) - f\big(v(x)\big)\,v'(x)$$ Example: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^3}\cos(t^2)\,dt = 3x^2\cos(x^6)$.
Reading $g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ from the graph of $f$ (three levels):
• $g(x)$ = signed area under $f$ from $a$ to $x$ (use geometry: triangles, trapezoids, semicircles).
• $g'(x) = f(x)$: $g$ increases where $f > 0$; relative max of $g$ where $f$ changes from $+$ to $-$.
• $g''(x) = f'(x)$: $g$ is concave up where $f$ is increasing; inflection points of $g$ occur where $f$ changes from increasing to decreasing (relative extrema of $f$).
• $g(a) = 0$ always; for $x \lt a$, $g(x) = -\int_x^a f(t)\,dt$.
Common Traps: (1) Forgetting the chain-rule factor $u'(x)$. (2) Differentiating the integrand instead of evaluating it at the limit. (3) Confusing the maximum of $f$ with the maximum of $g$ — the extrema of $g$ are found where $f = 0$ and changes sign, not where $f$ is largest.
6.6 & 6.7 Properties of Definite Integrals & FTC Part 2 Topic 6.6 & 6.7
Properties of Definite Integrals:
• $\int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0$ and $\int_b^a f(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$
• Additivity: $\int_a^c f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx + \int_b^c f(x)\,dx$
• Linearity: $\int_a^b \big[k f(x) \pm g(x)\big]dx = k\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \pm \int_a^b g(x)\,dx$
• Constants: $\int_a^b c\,dx = c(b - a)$
• Symmetry: $f$ odd ⟹ $\int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx = 0$; $f$ even ⟹ $\int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx = 2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx$
FTC Part 2 (Evaluation Theorem): If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $F' = f$, then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$.
Net Change form (the FRQ workhorse): $$F(b) = F(a) + \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx$$ Amount at time $b$ = amount at time $a$ + accumulated change. For tanks: $W(t) = W(0) + \int_0^t \big[\text{rate in} - \text{rate out}\big]\,ds$.
Common Traps: (1) Reporting the net change $\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx$ when the question asks for the amount $F(b)$ — always add the initial value. (2) Applying FTC 2 across a vertical asymptote: $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2} \ne -2$ (the integrand is not continuous on $[-1, 1]$; the integral is improper and diverges).
6.8 Antiderivatives & Indefinite Integrals: Basic Rules Topic 6.8
Essential Antiderivative Table (always add $+\,C$):
• $\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \;(n \ne -1)$    • $\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| + C$
• $\int e^{kx}\,dx = \frac{1}{k}e^{kx} + C$    • $\int a^x\,dx = \frac{a^x}{\ln a} + C$
• $\int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x + C$    • $\int \cos x\,dx = \sin x + C$
• $\int \sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x + C$    • $\int \csc^2 x\,dx = -\cot x + C$
• $\int \sec x\tan x\,dx = \sec x + C$    • $\int \csc x\cot x\,dx = -\csc x + C$
• $\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \arcsin x + C$    • $\int \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \arctan x + C$
Common Traps: There is no product rule or quotient rule for integrals: $\int f g\,dx \ne \int f\,dx \cdot \int g\,dx$. Rewrite first (e.g. $\frac{x^2 + 1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{3/2} + x^{-1/2}$). Always verify an antiderivative by differentiating it.
6.9 & 6.10 u-Substitution, Long Division & Completing the Square Topic 6.9 & 6.10
u-Substitution Protocol:
1. Choose $u$ = the "inside" function whose derivative (up to a constant) also appears.
2. Compute $du = u'(x)\,dx$ and solve for the piece of the integrand you need (e.g. $x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}du$).
3. If any $x$ remains, back-substitute from the definition of $u$ (e.g. $u = x - 1 \Rightarrow x = u + 1$).
4. Definite integrals: change the limits to $u$-values ($x = a \Rightarrow u = u(a)$) and never return to $x$; or return to $x$ and use the original limits — never mix the two.
Example: $\int_1^e \frac{(\ln x)^2}{x}\,dx = \int_0^1 u^2\,du = \frac{1}{3}$.
Long Division (Topic 6.10): If $\deg P \ge \deg Q$, divide first: $\frac{x^2 + 2x + 3}{x + 1} = x + 1 + \frac{2}{x + 1}$, so the integral is $\frac{x^2}{2} + x + 2\ln|x + 1| + C$.
Completing the Square (Topic 6.10): $\int \frac{dx}{x^2 + 4x + 13} = \int \frac{dx}{(x + 2)^2 + 3^2} = \frac{1}{3}\arctan\!\left(\frac{x + 2}{3}\right) + C$, using $\int \frac{du}{u^2 + a^2} = \frac{1}{a}\arctan\frac{u}{a} + C$.
Common Trap — Unchanged Limits: Evaluating a $u$-antiderivative at the original $x$-limits is the single most frequent u-substitution error on the AP exam. Write the new limits explicitly next to the integral sign.
6.11 & 6.12 Integration by Parts & Linear Partial Fractions BC ONLY Topic 6.11 & 6.12
Integration by Parts (IBP): $$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du \qquad \int_a^b u\,dv = uv\Big|_a^b - \int_a^b v\,du$$ Choosing $u$ (LIATE heuristic): Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential — pick $u$ from the earliest category present.
Example: $\int xe^{3x}\,dx$: $u = x$, $dv = e^{3x}dx$, $v = \frac{1}{3}e^{3x}$ ⟹ $\frac{x}{3}e^{3x} - \frac{1}{9}e^{3x} + C$.
Tabular method: for $x^n e^{kx}$, $x^n \sin(kx)$, repeatedly differentiate $u$ and integrate $dv$, alternating signs $+, -, +, \dots$
Partial Fractions (non-repeating linear factors only on the AP exam):
$\frac{5x + 1}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{B}{x + 2}$ ⟹ $5x + 1 = A(x + 2) + B(x - 1)$.
$x = 1$: $A = 2$; $x = -2$: $B = 3$ ⟹ $\int = 2\ln|x - 1| + 3\ln|x + 2| + C$.
Common Traps: (1) Dropping the minus sign in $uv - \int v\,du$. (2) Writing $v = 3e^{3x}$ instead of $\frac{1}{3}e^{3x}$ (you integrate $dv$). (3) Attempting partial fractions before long division when the numerator degree is not smaller than the denominator degree.
6.13 Improper Integrals & 6.14 Selecting Techniques BC ONLY (6.13) Topic 6.13 & 6.14
Two Types of Improper Integrals (always written with limits):
• Infinite interval: $\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. Example: $\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2}dx = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-b^2}\right] = \frac{1}{2}$.
• Infinite discontinuity: at an endpoint use a one-sided limit, e.g. $\int_0^4 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{4 - x}} = \lim_{b \to 4^-}\left[-2\sqrt{4 - x}\right]_0^b = 4$; at an interior point, split the integral and evaluate each piece separately.
• The integral converges if the limit is a finite number; otherwise it diverges.
• $p$-integrals: $\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^p}$ converges iff $p > 1$; $\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x^p}$ converges iff $p \lt 1$.
Topic 6.14 — Technique Selection Checklist: (1) Basic rule after algebraic rewriting? (2) Inside function with its derivative present ⟹ u-substitution. (3) Improper rational function ⟹ long division. (4) Irreducible quadratic denominator ⟹ complete the square ⟹ arctan. (5) BC: product of unlike function types ⟹ IBP. (6) BC: factorable denominator with distinct linear factors ⟹ partial fractions.
Rubric Trap: Writing "$= -\frac{1}{2}e^{-\infty} + \frac{1}{2}$" (treating $\infty$ as a number) loses the limit-notation point. Write $\lim_{b \to \infty}$ on every line until the limit is evaluated.
Unit 6 FRQ Writing Standards: Tables, Units & "Justify" Language FRQ Scoring • Communication
1. Riemann sums from tables: Show the full setup with every product, e.g. "$3(14) + 1(13) + 4(9)$". A bare numerical answer with no setup usually earns no setup point. Do not simplify arithmetic on the no-calculator section beyond what is needed.
2. Units: The units of $\int_a^b r(t)\,dt$ are (units of $r$) × (units of $t$). Include units whenever the question says "indicate units of measure."
3. Interpretation template: "$\int_2^5 R(t)\,dt = 40$ means that a total of 40 gallons of oil leaked from the pipeline over the time interval $2 \le t \le 5$ hours." Mention the quantity, the interval, and the units.
4. Net change / amount: Write the complete expression first: "$W(6) = 120 + \int_0^6 \big[E(t) - L(t)\big]\,dt$", then the calculator value to three decimal places.
5. "Justify" with accumulation functions: "$g$ has a relative maximum at $x = 2$ because $g'(x) = f(x)$ changes from positive to negative at $x = 2$." For absolute extrema, show a Candidates Test with all endpoint and critical values.
6. Over/underestimate: "The left Riemann sum is an underestimate because $r$ is increasing on the interval." The reason must refer to the behavior of the function, not to the picture.

A differential equation (DE) states how a quantity changes; solving it recovers the quantity itself. This unit builds the language of modeling with rates, checks candidate solutions, reads slope fields, and solves separable equations exactly — including the exponential growth/decay model behind population, cooling, and radioactive decay problems. 7.1 & 7.2 Modeling Situations with Differential Equations & Verifying Solutions Topic 7.1 & 7.2

Definition: A differential equation is an equation that contains a derivative of an unknown function. A solution is a function that makes the equation true on an interval. The general solution contains an arbitrary constant $C$ (a family of curves); a particular solution is the single member that satisfies an initial condition such as $y(0) = 4$.

Translation Dictionary (Topic 7.1):
• "The rate of change of $y$ is proportional to $y$" ⟹ $\frac{dy}{dt} = ky$
• "...proportional to the square root of $y$" ⟹ $\frac{dy}{dt} = k\sqrt{y}$
• "...inversely proportional to $y^2$" ⟹ $\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{k}{y^2}$
• "...proportional to the difference between $T$ and the room temperature $20$" ⟹ $\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - 20)$ (Newton's Law of Cooling)
• "...jointly proportional to $P$ and to $(M - P)$" ⟹ $\frac{dP}{dt} = kP(M - P)$ (logistic, BC)
The phrase "rate of change of $y$" always becomes the derivative $\frac{dy}{dt}$, never $y$ itself.
Verifying a Solution (Topic 7.2): Differentiate the candidate and substitute both sides.
Example: Is $y = 3x^2$ a solution of $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y}{x}$ for $x > 0$? Left side: $\frac{dy}{dx} = 6x$. Right side: $\frac{2(3x^2)}{x} = 6x$. Both sides agree, so yes.
Example: For which $k$ does $y = e^{kx}$ satisfy $y'' - y' - 6y = 0$? Substituting gives $e^{kx}(k^2 - k - 6) = 0$, so $k = 3$ or $k = -2$.
Common Trap: Checking only the initial condition is not verification. A function can pass through the right point and still fail the differential equation — you must substitute into the DE itself.
7.3 & 7.4 Sketching Slope Fields & Reasoning Using Slope Fields Topic 7.3 & 7.4

Core idea: At each grid point $(x, y)$, the DE $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$ gives the slope of the solution curve passing through that point. Drawing a short segment with that slope at many points produces a slope field. Every solution curve is tangent to the segments it passes through.

Worked slope field: $\frac{dy}{dx} = x - y$ on the integer grid $-3 \le x \le 3$, $-3 \le y \le 3$. Note the horizontal segments along the line $y = x$ (where $x - y = 0$) and the segments of slope $1$ along $y = x - 1$.

Slope Field Reading Protocol:
1. Where are the segments horizontal? Set $f(x, y) = 0$. For $x - y$, this is the line $y = x$.
2. Does the slope depend on $x$ only, $y$ only, or both? If every segment in a vertical column is identical, $f$ depends only on $x$; if every segment in a horizontal row is identical, $f$ depends only on $y$ (an autonomous DE).
3. Test one point with a nonzero slope (e.g. $(1, -1)$: $x - y = 2$, a steep positive segment) to separate answer choices.
4. Vertical segments mean the slope is undefined (a zero denominator), e.g. $y = 0$ for $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$.
Reasoning Without Solving (Topic 7.4):
• Equilibrium solutions: constant functions $y = c$ with $f(x, c) = 0$ for all $x$. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = x(y^2 - 9)$, these are $y = 3$ and $y = -3$.
• Concavity: differentiate the DE implicitly. For $\frac{dy}{dx} = x - y$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 1 - \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 - x + y$. Always substitute $\frac{dy}{dx}$ back in.
• Long-run behavior: for $\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 - y$, solutions above $y = 1$ decrease toward $1$ and solutions below increase toward $1$, so $\lim_{x \to \infty} y = 1$ for every solution.
Common Traps: (1) Swapping coordinates — the slope at $(2, 1)$ uses $x = 2$, $y = 1$. (2) Treating $y$ as a constant when computing $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$: $\frac{d}{dx}[y] = \frac{dy}{dx}$, not $0$. (3) Drawing a solution curve that crosses an equilibrium line — solutions of these AP-level equations never cross each other.
7.5 Approximating Solutions Using Euler's Method BC ONLY Topic 7.5
Euler's Method: Starting from $(x_0, y_0)$ with step size $h = \Delta x$: $$x_{n+1} = x_n + h, \qquad y_{n+1} = y_n + h \cdot f(x_n, y_n)$$ Each step follows the tangent line at the current point for a horizontal distance $h$.
Example: $\frac{dy}{dx} = x + y$, $y(0) = 1$, $h = 0.5$.
Step 1: slope at $(0, 1)$ is $1$ ⟹ $y(0.5) \approx 1 + 0.5(1) = 1.5$.
Step 2: slope at $(0.5, 1.5)$ is $2$ ⟹ $y(1) \approx 1.5 + 0.5(2) = 2.5$.
(The exact solution is $y = 2e^x - x - 1$, so $y(1) = 2e - 2 \approx 3.437$.)
Over- or Underestimate? Euler's method follows tangent lines. If the solution is concave up on the interval, tangent lines lie below the curve ⟹ Euler underestimates. If concave down ⟹ Euler overestimates. Find concavity from $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ computed implicitly from the DE. In the example above, $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 1 + \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 + x + y > 0$ along the path, so $2.5$ is an underestimate.
Common Traps: (1) Forgetting to multiply the slope by $h$. (2) Updating $x$ but evaluating the next slope at the old $y$. (3) Taking one large step when the problem specifies two steps. Present the work as a table ($x_n$, $y_n$, slope, $\Delta y$) to earn full credit.
7.6 & 7.7 Separation of Variables: General & Particular Solutions Topic 7.6 & 7.7
Separation of Variables Protocol: For $\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y)$:
1. Separate: $\frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx$ (all $y$ with $dy$, all $x$ with $dx$).
2. Integrate both sides and write $+\,C$ immediately: $\int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,dx + C$.
3. Use the initial condition right away to find $C$ (it is usually easiest before solving for $y$).
4. Solve for $y$ and state the domain: the largest open interval containing the initial $x$-value on which the solution is defined and differentiable.
Worked Example (Topic 7.7): Solve $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2xy$ with $y(0) = 3$.
$\frac{dy}{y} = 2x\,dx$ ⟹ $\ln|y| = x^2 + C$.
$x = 0, y = 3$: $\ln 3 = 0 + C$ ⟹ $C = \ln 3$.
$|y| = e^{x^2 + \ln 3} = 3e^{x^2}$. Since $y(0) = 3 > 0$, take the positive branch: $y = 3e^{x^2}$ for all real $x$.
Domain Example: $\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2$, $y(0) = 1$: $-\frac{1}{y} = x + C$, $C = -1$, so $y = \frac{1}{1 - x}$. The formula is undefined at $x = 1$, and the solution must live on an interval containing the initial value $x = 0$. Domain: $x \lt 1$.
Sign Example: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x}{y}$, $y(0) = -3$: $\frac{y^2}{2} = \frac{x^2}{2} + C$ ⟹ $y^2 = x^2 + 9$. Because $y(0) \lt 0$, choose $y = -\sqrt{x^2 + 9}$.
7.8 Exponential Models with Differential Equations Topic 7.8
The Exponential Model: $\frac{dy}{dt} = ky$ with $y(0) = y_0$ has the unique solution $$y = y_0 e^{kt}.$$ • $k > 0$: exponential growth; doubling time $\frac{\ln 2}{k}$.
• $k \lt 0$: exponential decay; half-life $\frac{\ln 2}{|k|}$.
• $k$ is the relative growth rate: $\frac{dy/dt}{y} = k$. For $\frac{dP}{dt} = 0.03P$, the population grows at $3\%$ of its current size per year.
Shifted Model (Newton's Law of Cooling): $\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - A)$ with ambient temperature $A$ gives $T = A + (T_0 - A)e^{kt}$.
Example: $\frac{dT}{dt} = -0.1(T - 20)$, $T(0) = 90$ ⟹ $T(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.1t}$, so $T(10) = 20 + 70e^{-1} \approx 45.75$.
Finding $k$ from two data points: If $y(0) = 50$ and $y(3) = 400$, then $e^{3k} = 8$ ⟹ $k = \frac{\ln 8}{3} = \ln 2$. Hence $y(t) = 50 \cdot 2^t$ and $y(5) = 1600$.
Common Traps: (1) Using $C = T_0$ instead of $C = T_0 - A$ in the cooling model. (2) Solving $e^{kt} = \frac{1}{2}$ as $t = \frac{\ln(1/2)}{k}$ and then dropping the sign of $k$ — a half-life is always positive. (3) Assuming a linear pattern ($50 \to 400$ in 3 years means $+116.7$ per year) when the model is exponential.
7.9 Logistic Models with Differential Equations BC ONLY Topic 7.9
Logistic DE: $$\frac{dP}{dt} = kP\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right) \quad\text{or equivalently}\quad \frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{k}{M}P(M - P)$$ • $M$ = carrying capacity. For any $P(0) > 0$: $\lim_{t \to \infty} P(t) = M$.
• If $0 \lt P(0) \lt M$, $P$ increases toward $M$; if $P(0) > M$, $P$ decreases toward $M$.
• The population grows fastest when $P = \frac{M}{2}$ (the inflection point of the solution curve when $P(0) \lt \frac{M}{2}$).
• Equilibrium solutions: $P = 0$ (unstable) and $P = M$ (stable).
Reading the Form: In $\frac{dP}{dt} = 0.4P\left(1 - \frac{P}{500}\right)$, $M = 500$ and the fastest growth occurs at $P = 250$. In $\frac{dy}{dt} = 2y(6 - y)$, rewrite as $12y\left(1 - \frac{y}{6}\right)$: $M = 6$, not $12$. The carrying capacity is the nonzero value of the population that makes the right-hand side zero.
Common Trap: "Where is the population growing fastest?" asks for the value of $P$ ($\frac{M}{2}$); "What is the maximum rate of growth?" asks for $\frac{dP}{dt}$ evaluated at $P = \frac{M}{2}$. Read the question stem carefully.
Unit 7 FRQ Writing Standards: Where Separation of Variables Loses Points FRQ Scoring • Separable DE Rubric
How a typical separable-DE FRQ part is scored (often 5–6 points): separation of variables, antiderivatives, constant of integration, use of the initial condition, solving for $y$. On the AP rubric, a solution with no constant of integration or no separation usually caps the score at the first one or two points — the rest of the work cannot be earned.
1. Write $+\,C$ at the moment you integrate — never later.
Wrong: $\ln|y| = \frac{x^2}{2}$ ⟹ $y = e^{x^2/2} + C$. This "late $C$" produces a function that does not satisfy the DE ($\frac{d}{dx}\big[e^{x^2/2} + 2\big] = xe^{x^2/2} \ne x\big(e^{x^2/2} + 2\big)$).
Right: $\ln|y| = \frac{x^2}{2} + C$ ⟹ $|y| = e^{C}e^{x^2/2}$ ⟹ $y = Ae^{x^2/2}$ (the constant is multiplicative after exponentiating).
2. Keep the absolute value: $\int \frac{dy}{y} = \ln|y| + C$. Resolve the sign with the initial condition: if $y(0) = -2$ for $\frac{dy}{dx} = y\cos x$, then $y = -2e^{\sin x}$. Writing $\ln y$ and declaring "no solution because $\ln(-2)$ is undefined" is a costly error.
3. Use the initial condition correctly. Substitute both coordinates into the equation that contains $C$; for $\ln|y| = x^2 + C$ with $y(0) = 3$, $C = \ln 3$, not $C = 3$. When the solution involves a square root, keep the branch that matches the sign of $y_0$.
4. Shifted variables: for $\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2(y - 1)$, integrate to $\ln|y - 1| = \frac{x^3}{3} + C$; the "$-1$" stays with $y$ throughout.
5. State the domain when asked: the open interval containing the initial $x$-value on which the solution is defined.
Language Note: Distinguish the general solution (a family, contains $C$) from the particular solution (one function, no $C$). Say "the solution curve is tangent to the slope field," not "the solution follows the arrows" — slope fields contain line segments, not arrows. "Proportional to" introduces a constant $k$; "inversely proportional to" puts the quantity in the denominator.

AP Calculus AB/BC — Unit 8: Applications of Integration

Unit 6 taught you how to compute a definite integral; Unit 8 teaches you what a definite integral measures. The same idea — slice, approximate each slice, add the slices with an integral — produces average values, displacement and total distance, accumulated amounts, areas between curves, and volumes of solids. Unit 8 carries 10–15% of the AP Calculus AB exam score and 5–10% of the BC score (College Board AP Central, 2026-27), and area/volume questions appear on the free-response section almost every year. Topics 8.1–8.12 are tested on both AB and BC; the section marked BC ONLY (Topic 8.13 Arc Length) is tested only on AP Calculus BC.

8.1 Finding the Average Value of a Function on an Interval Topic 8.1

Definition: If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, the average value of $f$ on $[a, b]$ is $$f_{avg} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$ Geometrically, $f_{avg}$ is the height of the rectangle on $[a, b]$ whose area equals the (signed) area under $f$.

Mean Value Theorem for Integrals: If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, there is at least one $c$ in $(a, b)$ with $f(c) = f_{avg}$. On an FRQ, state the hypothesis ("$f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$") before you use the conclusion.
Example: $f(x) = x^2$ on $[1, 4]$: $\int_1^4 x^2\,dx = 21$, so $f_{avg} = \frac{21}{3} = 7$, attained at $c = \sqrt{7} \approx 2.646$.
Three Different "Averages": (1) Average value of $f$: $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. (2) Average rate of change of $f$: $\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$ — this is the average value of $f'$. (3) The mean of the endpoint values $\frac{f(a) + f(b)}{2}$ — equal to the average value only for linear functions. Read which one the stem asks for.
8.2 Connecting Position, Velocity, and Acceleration of Functions Using Integrals Topic 8.2
The Integral Chain: integration reverses the derivative chain $x \to v \to a$.
• Velocity from acceleration: $v(t) = v(0) + \int_0^t a(s)\,ds$.
• Position from velocity: $x(t) = x(0) + \int_0^t v(s)\,ds$.
• Displacement on $[a, b]$: $\int_a^b v(t)\,dt$ (signed; can be negative or zero).
• Total distance traveled on $[a, b]$: $\int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt$ (never negative).
• Average velocity on $[a, b]$: $\frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b v(t)\,dt$.
Worked Example: $v(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3 = (t - 1)(t - 3)$ on $[0, 4]$.
Displacement: $\int_0^4 v(t)\,dt = \frac{4}{3}$.
Distance: split at the sign changes $t = 1$ and $t = 3$: $\left|\frac{4}{3}\right| + \left|-\frac{4}{3}\right| + \left|\frac{4}{3}\right| = 4$.
On a calculator-active question, simply enter $\int_0^4 |v(t)|\,dt$ directly.
Common Traps: (1) Forgetting the initial position: $\int_0^2 v(t)\,dt$ is a change in position, not the position. (2) Reporting displacement when the stem says "total distance." (3) Treating speed $|v(t)|$ and velocity $v(t)$ as interchangeable.
8.3 Using Accumulation Functions and Definite Integrals in Applied Contexts Topic 8.3

Net Change Theorem: if $r(t)$ is the rate of change of a quantity $Q$, then $\int_a^b r(t)\,dt = Q(b) - Q(a)$ is the net change in $Q$ from $t = a$ to $t = b$. Units: (units of $r$) × (units of $t$) — e.g. (gallons/minute) × (minutes) = gallons.

The Tank Template: water enters at $R(t)$ and leaves at $D(t)$; the tank holds $A_0$ at $t = 0$. $$A(t) = A_0 + \int_0^t [R(s) - D(s)]\,ds, \qquad A'(t) = R(t) - D(t).$$ • "Is the amount increasing at $t = 3$?" ⟹ check the sign of $R(3) - D(3)$.
• "When is the amount a maximum on $[0, T]$?" ⟹ Candidates Test: evaluate $A$ at $t = 0$, $t = T$, and every $t$ where $R(t) = D(t)$.
Example: $E(t) = 12$, $L(t) = 3t$, $A(0) = 100$: $A'(t) = 0$ at $t = 4$; $A(0) = 100$, $A(4) = 124$, $A(6) = 118$, so the maximum is $124$.
Interpretation Sentence (FRQ): "$\int_2^5 E(t)\,dt$ is the total number of people who enter the park from $t = 2$ to $t = 5$ hours." Include the quantity, the units, and the time interval. Do not describe an integral of a rate as a rate.
8.4–8.6 Finding the Area Between Curves Topics 8.4, 8.5 & 8.6

Vertical slices (Topic 8.4, functions of $x$): a slice at $x$ has height $f(x) - g(x)$ (top minus bottom) and width $dx$: $$A = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)]\,dx, \quad f(x) \ge g(x) \text{ on } [a, b].$$ The limits $a$ and $b$ are usually intersection points: for $y = 2x$ and $y = x^2$, solve $2x = x^2$ to get $x = 0$ and $x = 2$, so $A = \int_0^2 (2x - x^2)\,dx = \frac{4}{3}$.

Horizontal slices (Topic 8.5, functions of $y$): when the boundaries are naturally written $x = g(y)$, or when vertical slices would need two integrals, slice horizontally: $$A = \int_c^d [x_{right}(y) - x_{left}(y)]\,dy.$$ Example: $x = y^2$ and $x = y + 2$ meet at $y = -1$ and $y = 2$; $A = \int_{-1}^{2} (y + 2 - y^2)\,dy = \frac{9}{2}$. With vertical slices the lower boundary would change at $x = 1$ and two integrals would be required.
More than two intersections (Topic 8.6): find every intersection, test which curve is on top in each subinterval, and add the pieces — equivalently, $A = \int_a^b |f(x) - g(x)|\,dx$.
Example: $y = x^3$ and $y = x$ meet at $x = -1, 0, 1$. $\int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x)\,dx + \int_0^1 (x - x^3)\,dx = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$, while the single integral $\int_{-1}^{1} (x - x^3)\,dx$ incorrectly gives $0$.
Common Traps: (1) A negative "area" means the subtraction order is reversed. (2) Using $x$-limits for a $dy$ integral (or the reverse) — convert the limits with the curve equations. (3) Integrating across a crossing point with one integrand, so the pieces cancel.
8.7–8.8 Volumes with Cross Sections Topics 8.7 & 8.8

Core idea: if every cross section perpendicular to the $x$-axis has area $A(x)$, then $V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx$. The side length $s(x)$ of each cross section is the length of the slice in the base: $s(x) = (\text{top}) - (\text{bottom})$. For sections perpendicular to the $y$-axis, use $s(y) = x_{right} - x_{left}$ and $V = \int_c^d A(y)\,dy$.

Cross-Section Area Formulas (side $s$ lies in the base):
• Square: $A = s^2$ (Topic 8.7)
• Rectangle with height $k$ times its base: $A = k s^2$ (Topic 8.7)
• Isosceles right triangle with a leg in the base: $A = \frac{1}{2}s^2$ (Topic 8.8)
• Isosceles right triangle with the hypotenuse in the base: $A = \frac{1}{4}s^2$ (Topic 8.8)
• Equilateral triangle: $A = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2$ (Topic 8.8)
• Semicircle with diameter $s$ in the base: $A = \frac{1}{2}\pi\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\pi}{8}s^2$ (Topic 8.8)
Worked Example: base bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, the $x$-axis, and $x = 4$; square cross sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis. $s(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $A(x) = x$, $V = \int_0^4 x\,dx = 8$. With semicircles instead: $V = \frac{\pi}{8}\int_0^4 x\,dx = \pi$.
Common Traps: (1) Writing $\pi$ for a square or triangle cross section — $\pi$ appears only with circles and semicircles. (2) Using the diameter as the radius in a semicircle. (3) Expanding $(f - g)^2$ as $f^2 - g^2$.
8.9–8.12 Volumes of Revolution: The Disc and Washer Methods Topics 8.9–8.12
Disc Method (Topics 8.9 & 8.10): when the region touches the axis of rotation, each slice perpendicular to the axis sweeps out a solid disc of radius $R$: $$V = \pi\int_a^b [R(x)]^2\,dx \quad \text{(horizontal axis)}, \qquad V = \pi\int_c^d [R(y)]^2\,dy \quad \text{(vertical axis)}.$$ • About the $x$-axis: $y = x^2$, $0 \le x \le 2$ gives $V = \pi\int_0^2 x^4\,dx = \frac{32\pi}{5}$.
• About the $y$-axis: slice horizontally and write $x$ in terms of $y$. For $x = \sqrt{y}$, $0 \le y \le 4$: $V = \pi\int_0^4 y\,dy = 8\pi$.
• About another line (Topic 8.10): $R = |\text{axis} - \text{curve}|$. Region between $y = x^2$ and $y = 4$ about $y = 4$: $R = 4 - x^2$, $V = \pi\int_{-2}^{2} (4 - x^2)^2\,dx = \frac{512\pi}{15}$.
Washer Method (Topics 8.11 & 8.12): when there is a gap between the region and the axis, each slice is a washer with outer radius $R$ (the boundary farther from the axis) and inner radius $r$ (the boundary closer to the axis): $$V = \pi\int_a^b \left([R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2\right)dx.$$ • About the $x$-axis: region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$: $R = x$, $r = x^2$, $V = \pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx = \frac{2\pi}{15}$.
• About $y = 2$ (above the region): distances are measured downward from the axis, so $R = 2 - x^2$ (the lower curve is farther) and $r = 2 - x$: $V = \pi\int_0^1 \left[(2 - x^2)^2 - (2 - x)^2\right]dx = \frac{8\pi}{15}$.
• About $y = -1$ (below the region): $R = (\text{top}) + 1$, $r = (\text{bottom}) + 1$.
Radius Protocol: sketch the axis, draw one representative slice perpendicular to it, and write each radius as (larger coordinate) − (smaller coordinate). Never write $\pi(R - r)^2$: the washer area is $\pi R^2 - \pi r^2$. The shell method is not part of the AP Calculus CED; every AP volume question can be done with discs, washers, or cross sections.
8.13 The Arc Length of a Smooth, Planar Curve and Distance Traveled BC ONLY Topic 8.13
Arc Length: if $f'$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, a tiny piece of the curve has length $\sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\,dx$, so $$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\,dx.$$ Example: $y = \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}$ on $[0, 3]$: $f'(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $L = \int_0^3 \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx = \left[\frac{2}{3}(1 + x)^{3/2}\right]_0^3 = \frac{14}{3}$.
Common Traps: (1) Dropping the $1$ under the root. (2) Squaring $f(x)$ instead of $f'(x)$. (3) Expecting a closed form: most arc-length integrals on the AP exam are calculator-active and are evaluated numerically. For curves $x = g(y)$, use $L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + [g'(y)]^2}\,dy$.

Distinguish "displacement" (net change in position) from "total distance traveled," and "average value" from "average rate of change."

9.1 Defining and Differentiating Parametric Equations Topic 9.1

Definition: a parametric curve is a pair of functions $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$; as the parameter $t$ increases, the point $(x(t), y(t))$ traces the curve in a definite direction (the orientation). If $x$ and $y$ are differentiable and $\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0$, the chain rule $\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}$ gives $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}.$$

Worked Example: $x(t) = t^2$, $y(t) = t^3 - 3t$.
• $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t$, $\frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t}$. At $t = 2$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{9}{4}$, and the point is $(4, 2)$, so the tangent line is $y - 2 = \frac{9}{4}(x - 4)$.
• Horizontal tangent: $\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0$ ⟹ $t = \pm 1$, at the points $(1, -2)$ and $(1, 2)$.
• Vertical tangent: $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} \ne 0$ ⟹ $t = 0$, at the point $(0, 0)$.
Common Traps: (1) Inverting the quotient: the numerator is always the derivative of the output $y$. (2) Writing the tangent line with $t$ instead of the point $(x(t_0), y(t_0))$ — the line lives in the $xy$-plane. (3) Setting $x(t) = 0$ instead of $x'(t) = 0$ when looking for a vertical tangent. If both $\frac{dx}{dt}$ and $\frac{dy}{dt}$ are $0$, the test is inconclusive and a limit is needed.
9.2 Second Derivatives of Parametric Equations Topic 9.2

The second derivative is the derivative of the slope function with respect to $x$. Because $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is written in terms of $t$, apply the same quotient rule again: $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}.$$

Worked Example (continued): $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t} = \frac{3t}{2} - \frac{3}{2t}$.
• $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{3}{2t^2}$.
• Divide by $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{3(t^2 + 1)}{4t^3}$. At $t = 2$: $\frac{15}{32} \gt 0$, so the curve is concave up there.
Common Traps: (1) Stopping after $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)$ — that is a rate with respect to $t$, not $x$. (2) Computing $\frac{y''(t)}{x''(t)}$; this ratio is not the second derivative. (3) Testing the sign of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ when the question asks about concavity.
9.3 Finding Arc Lengths of Curves Given by Parametric Equations Topic 9.3

Over a short time $dt$ the point moves $dx = x'(t)\,dt$ horizontally and $dy = y'(t)\,dt$ vertically, so by Pythagoras the piece of curve has length $\sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}\,dt$. If $x'$ and $y'$ are continuous and the curve is traced once for $a \le t \le b$, $$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt.$$

Worked Example (one arch of a cycloid): $x = t - \sin t$, $y = 1 - \cos t$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$.
$(x')^2 + (y')^2 = (1 - \cos t)^2 + \sin^2 t = 2 - 2\cos t = 4\sin^2\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)$, and $\sin\left(\frac{t}{2}\right) \ge 0$ on $[0, 2\pi]$, so $L = \int_0^{2\pi} 2\sin\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)dt = \left[-4\cos\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\right]_0^{2\pi} = 8$.
On a calculator-active question, enter the integral directly: fnInt gives $8.000$.
Common Traps: (1) Leaving out the square root, i.e. integrating $(x')^2 + (y')^2$. (2) Integrating $|x'| + |y'|$ — that is a staircase, not the curve. (3) Reporting the straight-line distance between the endpoints. (4) Using a $t$-interval on which the curve is traced more than once.
9.4 Defining and Differentiating Vector-Valued Functions Topic 9.4

A vector-valued function $\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t) \rangle$ is a parametric curve written as one object: the position vector. Its derivatives are taken component by component: $$\vec{v}(t) = \vec{r}\,'(t) = \langle x'(t), y'(t) \rangle, \qquad \vec{a}(t) = \vec{v}\,'(t) = \langle x''(t), y''(t) \rangle.$$ The speed is the magnitude of velocity: $|\vec{v}(t)| = \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}$. The slope of the path is still $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y'(t)}{x'(t)}$.

Worked Example: $\vec{r}(t) = \langle t^3 - 3t,\ t^2 \rangle$.
• $\vec{v}(t) = \langle 3t^2 - 3,\ 2t \rangle$, $\vec{a}(t) = \langle 6t,\ 2 \rangle$.
• At $t = 2$: $\vec{v}(2) = \langle 9, 4 \rangle$, speed $= \sqrt{97}$, $\vec{a}(2) = \langle 12, 2 \rangle$.
• $\vec{v}(2) \cdot \vec{a}(2) = 108 + 8 = 116 \gt 0$, so the speed is increasing at $t = 2$.
Common Traps: (1) Reporting speed as $|x'| + |y'|$ or as $(x')^2 + (y')^2$ without the root. (2) Giving the magnitude of the position vector when the stem asks for speed. (3) Forgetting the chain rule inside a component: $\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\pi t) = -\pi\sin(\pi t)$.
9.5 Integrating Vector-Valued Functions Topic 9.5

$$\int_a^b \vec{v}(t)\,dt = \left\langle \int_a^b x'(t)\,dt,\ \int_a^b y'(t)\,dt \right\rangle, \qquad \vec{r}(b) = \vec{r}(a) + \int_a^b \vec{v}(t)\,dt.$$ The definite integral of velocity is the displacement vector; adding the initial position gives the new position. An indefinite integral carries a constant vector $\vec{C} = \langle C_1, C_2 \rangle$.

Worked Example: $\vec{v}(t) = \langle \cos t,\ 2t \rangle$ and $\vec{r}(0) = \langle 1, 0 \rangle$.
$\int_0^{\pi/2} \vec{v}(t)\,dt = \left\langle [\sin t]_0^{\pi/2},\ [t^2]_0^{\pi/2} \right\rangle = \left\langle 1,\ \frac{\pi^2}{4} \right\rangle$, so $\vec{r}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \left\langle 2,\ \frac{\pi^2}{4} \right\rangle$.
Common Traps: (1) Dropping the initial position (answering with the displacement). (2) Differentiating a component instead of integrating it. (3) Losing the chain-rule constant: $\int e^{2t}\,dt = \frac{1}{2}e^{2t} + C$.
9.6 Solving Motion Problems Using Parametric and Vector-Valued Functions Topic 9.6
The Planar Motion Toolkit:
• Moving right/left: sign of $x'(t)$. Moving up/down: sign of $y'(t)$.
• At rest: $x'(t) = 0$ and $y'(t) = 0$ at the same $t$.
• Speed increasing: $\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} \gt 0$ (decreasing if $\lt 0$). Equivalently, differentiate the speed $\sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}$.
• Position: $\vec{r}(b) = \vec{r}(a) + \int_a^b \vec{v}(t)\,dt$.
• Total distance traveled: $\int_a^b |\vec{v}(t)|\,dt = \int_a^b \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}\,dt$ — the same integral as Topic 9.3.
Worked Example: $\vec{v}(t) = \langle 3t^2 - 6t,\ 4 - 2t \rangle$ for $t \ge 0$.
$x'(t) = 0$ at $t = 0, 2$; $y'(t) = 0$ at $t = 2$. The particle is at rest only at $t = 2$. For $0 \lt t \lt 2$, $x'(t) \lt 0$ and $y'(t) \gt 0$: the particle moves left and up.
Common Traps: (1) Calling a particle "at rest" when only one component of velocity is zero (it is then moving purely vertically or horizontally). (2) Using the magnitude of the displacement vector for total distance. (3) Deciding "speeding up" from the sign of one acceleration component — the test needs the dot product of the full vectors. (4) Running the calculator in degree mode.
9.7 Defining Polar Coordinates and Differentiating in Polar Form Topic 9.7

A point with polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ has rectangular coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, and $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$. For a polar curve $r = f(\theta)$ this is a parametric curve with parameter $\theta$: $$x(\theta) = f(\theta)\cos\theta, \quad y(\theta) = f(\theta)\sin\theta, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta} = \frac{f'(\theta)\sin\theta + f(\theta)\cos\theta}{f'(\theta)\cos\theta - f(\theta)\sin\theta}.$$

Worked Example: $r = 2 + 2\sin\theta$ at $\theta = 0$: $r = 2$, $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = 2\cos\theta = 2$.
$\frac{dy}{d\theta} = 2(0) + 2(1) = 2$, $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = 2(1) - 2(0) = 2$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = 1$.
Interpreting $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$: when $r \gt 0$, $\frac{dr}{d\theta} \gt 0$ means the curve is moving away from the origin and $\frac{dr}{d\theta} \lt 0$ means it is moving toward the origin. When $r \lt 0$, these conclusions reverse.
Common Traps: (1) Reporting $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$ as the slope of the curve — it is the rate of change of the distance from the origin. (2) Product-rule sign error: $\frac{d}{d\theta}(r\cos\theta) = r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta$. (3) Assuming the tangent is vertical at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ just because $\tan\theta$ is undefined there.
9.8–9.9 Area of a Polar Region and Area Between Two Polar Curves Topics 9.8 & 9.9

Single curve (Topic 9.8): polar area is built from thin circular sectors of area $\frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta$, so the region swept from $\theta = \alpha$ to $\theta = \beta$ is $$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta [r(\theta)]^2\,d\theta.$$ Find the $\theta$-interval on which the region is traced exactly once — solve $r(\theta) = 0$ to find where loops and petals start and end.

Worked Example (Topic 9.8): one petal of $r = 2\sin(2\theta)$ is traced for $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$ ($r = 0$ at both ends): $A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\pi/2} 4\sin^2(2\theta)\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$. The four-petal rose encloses $4 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 2\pi$.
Two curves (Topic 9.9): if $R(\theta) \ge r(\theta) \ge 0$ on $[\alpha, \beta]$, the region inside $R$ and outside $r$ has area $$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta \left([R(\theta)]^2 - [r(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta.$$ Worked Example: inside $r = 4\sin\theta$ and outside $r = 2$. Intersections: $4\sin\theta = 2$ ⟹ $\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{5\pi}{6}$. $A = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\pi/6}^{5\pi/6} \left(16\sin^2\theta - 4\right)d\theta = \frac{4\pi}{3} + 2\sqrt{3} \approx 7.653$.
For a region inside both curves, split the $\theta$-interval at the intersection and, on each piece, use the curve that is closer to the origin.
Common Traps: (1) Forgetting the $\frac{1}{2}$. (2) Writing $\frac{1}{2}\int (R - r)^2\,d\theta$ instead of $\frac{1}{2}\int (R^2 - r^2)\,d\theta$ — the same washer mistake as Unit 8. (3) Integrating over $[0, 2\pi]$ for a curve such as $r = 1 + 2\sin\theta$ whose inner loop is then counted twice. (4) Integrating $\frac{1}{2}\int r\,d\theta$ without squaring.

AP Calculus BC — Unit 10: Infinite Sequences and Series BC ONLY

Unit 10 asks a question that no earlier unit could answer: what does it mean to add infinitely many numbers, and when does the answer exist? The first half of the unit (Topics 10.1–10.10) builds a toolbox of convergence tests; the second half (Topics 10.11–10.15) uses that toolbox to replace functions such as $e^x$, $\sin x$ and $\ln(1 + x)$ by polynomials and power series, and to measure how large the error of that replacement can be. The whole unit is tested only on AP Calculus BC and carries 15–20% of the BC exam score — the largest single unit on the exam (College Board AP Central, AP Calculus BC course page, 2026-27). Topics 10.1–10.15 below follow the current Course and Exam Description; a Taylor-polynomial / error-bound or interval-of-convergence free-response question appears on almost every BC exam.

10.1 Defining Convergent and Divergent Infinite Series Topic 10.1

Sequence vs. series: a sequence $\{a_n\}$ is an ordered list of numbers; a series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ is the attempt to add them. The series is given meaning through its sequence of partial sums $$S_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n.$$ If $\lim_{n\to\infty} S_n = S$ exists (as a finite number), the series converges and its sum is $S$; otherwise it diverges.

Worked Example (telescoping series): $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\right)$.
$S_n = \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \cdots + \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1}$, so $S_n \to 1$ and the series converges to $1$.
If instead the partial sums are given, e.g. $S_n = \frac{4n+1}{n+3}$, then the sum is $\lim S_n = 4$, while the first term is $a_1 = S_1 = \frac{5}{4}$.
Common Traps: (1) Confusing $\lim a_n$ (the terms) with $\lim S_n$ (the sum). (2) Assuming that every term cancels in a telescoping sum — count which terms survive at the start. (3) Believing that an increasing sequence of partial sums must diverge; an increasing sequence that is bounded above converges.
10.2 Working with Geometric Series Topic 10.2

A geometric series has a constant ratio $r$ between consecutive terms: $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a r^n = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1 - r} \quad \text{if } |r| \lt 1,$$ and it diverges if $|r| \ge 1$ (and $a \ne 0$). The numerator is always the first term actually written, whatever the starting index.

Worked Example: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 5\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n$: first term $5 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{10}{3}$, ratio $\frac{2}{3}$, sum $= \frac{10/3}{1/3} = 10$.
Alternating ratio: $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n 3^{n+1}}{4^n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 3\left(-\frac{3}{4}\right)^n = \frac{3}{1 + 3/4} = \frac{12}{7}$.
Common Traps: (1) Using the coefficient $a$ as the first term when the sum starts at $n = 1$. (2) Dropping the sign of a negative ratio: $1 - \left(-\frac{3}{4}\right) = \frac{7}{4}$, not $\frac{1}{4}$. (3) Applying $\frac{a}{1 - r}$ when $|r| \ge 1$ — the formula then gives a number, but the series diverges.
10.3 The nth Term Test for Divergence Topic 10.3

Statement: if $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \ne 0$ (or the limit does not exist), then $\sum a_n$ diverges. The reason: if the series converged, then $a_n = S_n - S_{n-1} \to S - S = 0$.

Worked Example: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n}{2n+1}$ diverges because $\lim \frac{n}{2n+1} = \frac{1}{2} \ne 0$.
Inconclusive case: $\lim \frac{1}{n} = 0$, yet the harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges; $\lim \frac{1}{n^2} = 0$, and $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges. A zero limit decides nothing.
Common Traps: (1) Writing "$a_n \to 0$, so the series converges by the nth term test" — this is the most heavily penalised sentence on Unit 10 FRQs. (2) Testing the limit of the partial sums instead of the terms. (3) Forgetting that the test applies to every series, including alternating ones: $\sum (-1)^n \frac{n}{n+1}$ diverges because its terms do not approach $0$.
10.4 Integral Test for Convergence · 10.5 Harmonic Series and p-Series Topics 10.4–10.5

Integral Test (10.4): let $a_n = f(n)$, where $f$ is positive, continuous and decreasing on $[k, \infty)$. Then $\sum_{n=k}^{\infty} a_n$ and $\int_k^{\infty} f(x)\,dx$ either both converge or both diverge. The test gives convergence, not the value of the sum.

Worked Example: $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}$. With $u = \ln x$: $\int_2^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^2} = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-\frac{1}{\ln x}\right]_2^b = \frac{1}{\ln 2}$, finite, so the series converges. (Its sum is about $2.04$, not $\frac{1}{\ln 2} \approx 1.44$.) By contrast, $\int_2^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x\ln x} = \lim \left[\ln(\ln x)\right]_2^b = \infty$, so $\sum \frac{1}{n\ln n}$ diverges.
p-Series (10.5): $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p}$ converges if $p \gt 1$ and diverges if $p \le 1$ (proved with the Integral Test). The harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ ($p = 1$) diverges even though its terms approach $0$.
Examples: $\sum \frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}$ ($p = \frac{3}{2}$) converges; $\sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ ($p = \frac{1}{2}$) diverges; $\sum \frac{1}{n^{2p-1}}$ converges exactly when $2p - 1 \gt 1$, i.e. $p \gt 1$.
Common Traps: (1) Reporting the value of the integral as the sum of the series. (2) Skipping the hypotheses — on an FRQ, state "positive, continuous and decreasing." (3) Treating $p = 1$ as convergent. (4) Confusing a p-series $\frac{1}{n^p}$ (variable base) with a geometric series $\frac{1}{p^n}$ (variable exponent).
10.6 Comparison Tests for Convergence Topic 10.6

Both tests apply to series with positive terms.
Direct Comparison: if $0 \lt a_n \le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges ("smaller than convergent"). If $0 \lt b_n \le a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ diverges, then $\sum a_n$ diverges ("larger than divergent").
Limit Comparison: if $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = L$ with $0 \lt L \lt \infty$, then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ both converge or both diverge.

Worked Examples:
• $\sum \frac{1}{n^2 + 5}$: $\frac{1}{n^2 + 5} \lt \frac{1}{n^2}$ and $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges ($p = 2$), so the series converges by Direct Comparison.
• $\sum \frac{3n + 1}{n^3 + 2}$: choose $b_n = \frac{1}{n^2}$ (keep the dominant powers: $\frac{3n}{n^3}$). $\lim \frac{a_n}{b_n} = \lim \frac{3n^3 + n^2}{n^3 + 2} = 3$, finite and positive, and $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges, so the series converges by Limit Comparison.
Common Traps: (1) Comparing in the wrong direction: "$\frac{1}{n^2 + 5} \lt \frac{1}{n}$ and $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges" proves nothing. (2) Choosing a comparison series that gives $L = 0$ or $L = \infty$; then the Limit Comparison Test is inconclusive. (3) Using comparison tests on series with negative terms — compare $|a_n|$ instead (Topic 10.9).
10.7 Alternating Series Test for Convergence Topic 10.7

Alternating Series Test (AST): a series $\sum (-1)^n b_n$ (or $\sum (-1)^{n+1} b_n$) converges if (i) $b_n \gt 0$, (ii) $b_{n+1} \le b_n$ for all $n$ (eventually), and (iii) $\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = 0$.

Worked Examples:
• $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{\ln n}$ ($n \ge 2$): $\frac{1}{\ln n}$ is positive, decreasing and tends to $0$, so the series converges by the AST.
• $\sum (-1)^n \frac{n}{n+1}$: $b_n \to 1 \ne 0$, so the AST does not apply and the series diverges by the nth term test.
Common Traps: (1) Concluding divergence because the AST "fails" — a failed AST proves nothing by itself; use the nth term test. (2) Omitting the "decreasing" condition in a written justification. (3) Mixing the AST with absolute convergence: the AST alone never tells you that $\sum |a_n|$ converges.
10.8 Ratio Test for Convergence Topic 10.8

Ratio Test: let $L = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|$. If $L \lt 1$, $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely; if $L \gt 1$ (or $L = \infty$), it diverges; if $L = 1$, the test is inconclusive. It is the test of choice when $a_n$ contains $n!$ or $c^n$.

Worked Examples:
• $\sum \frac{n^2}{3^n}$: $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \frac{(n+1)^2}{n^2}\cdot\frac{1}{3} \to \frac{1}{3} \lt 1$, so the series converges.
• $\sum \frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2\,5^n}$: $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \frac{(2n+2)(2n+1)}{(n+1)^2}\cdot\frac{1}{5} \to \frac{4}{5} \lt 1$, so the series converges.
• Any p-series gives $L = 1$: the Ratio Test cannot decide $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ or $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$.
Common Traps: (1) Inverting the ratio ($\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}$). (2) Simplifying $\frac{(2n+2)!}{(2n)!}$ as $2n + 2$ — two factors survive: $(2n+2)(2n+1)$. (3) Declaring convergence when $L = 1$.
10.9 Determining Absolute or Conditional Convergence Topic 10.9

A series is absolutely convergent if $\sum |a_n|$ converges; absolute convergence implies convergence. It is conditionally convergent if $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum |a_n|$ diverges.

Decision procedure: (1) Test $\sum |a_n|$ (p-series, comparison, ratio). If it converges ⟹ absolutely convergent. (2) If it diverges, test $\sum a_n$ itself (usually with the AST). If it converges ⟹ conditionally convergent; otherwise divergent.
Examples: $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt[3]{n}}$ is conditionally convergent ($\sum \frac{1}{n^{1/3}}$ diverges, AST holds). $\sum \frac{\sin n}{n^2}$ is absolutely convergent ($\frac{|\sin n|}{n^2} \le \frac{1}{n^2}$). $\sum (-1)^n \frac{n}{n^2 + 1}$ is conditionally convergent (Limit Comparison of $|a_n|$ with $\frac{1}{n}$ gives divergence; AST gives convergence).
Common Traps: (1) Calling a series "absolutely convergent" because it alternates. (2) Concluding divergence from the divergence of $\sum |a_n|$ — that only rules out absolute convergence. (3) Forgetting that a series of positive terms that converges is automatically absolutely convergent.
10.10 Alternating Series Error Bound Topic 10.10

If $\sum (-1)^{n+1} b_n$ satisfies the AST and $S$ is its sum, then the error of the partial sum $S_n$ is at most the first omitted term: $$|S - S_n| \le b_{n+1}.$$ Moreover, $S$ lies between consecutive partial sums $S_n$ and $S_{n+1}$.

Worked Examples:
• $S = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^3}$ approximated by $S_4$: $|S - S_4| \le b_5 = \frac{1}{125}$.
• How many terms guarantee $|S - S_n| \lt 0.001$ for $\sum \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}$? Need $\frac{1}{(n+1)^2} \lt 0.001$, i.e. $(n+1)^2 \gt 1000$. Since $31^2 = 961$ and $32^2 = 1024$, $n + 1 = 32$ and $n = 31$.
Common Traps: (1) Using the last term kept ($b_n$) instead of the first term omitted ($b_{n+1}$). (2) Rounding $\sqrt{1000} \approx 31.6$ down instead of up. (3) Applying this bound to a series that is not alternating or whose terms are not decreasing — use the Lagrange bound (Topic 10.12) instead.
10.11 Finding Taylor Polynomial Approximations of Functions Topic 10.11

The $n$th-degree Taylor polynomial for $f$ about $x = c$ matches $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at $c$: $$P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(c)}{k!}(x - c)^k = f(c) + f'(c)(x - c) + \frac{f''(c)}{2!}(x - c)^2 + \cdots + \frac{f^{(n)}(c)}{n!}(x - c)^n.$$ When $c = 0$ it is called a Maclaurin polynomial.

Worked Example: $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ about $x = 4$: $f(4) = 2$, $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ so $f'(4) = \frac{1}{4}$, $f''(x) = -\frac{1}{4x^{3/2}}$ so $f''(4) = -\frac{1}{32}$.
$P_2(x) = 2 + \frac{1}{4}(x - 4) - \frac{1}{64}(x - 4)^2$, and $P_2(4.2) = 2 + 0.05 - 0.000625 = 2.049375$ (true value $\sqrt{4.2} \approx 2.049390$).
Common Traps: (1) Forgetting to divide by $k!$. (2) Writing powers of $x$ instead of $(x - c)$ when $c \ne 0$. (3) Reading "the coefficient of $(x - c)^3$" as $f'''(c)$ — the coefficient is $\frac{f'''(c)}{3!}$; conversely, $f'''(c) = 3! \times$ coefficient.
10.12 Lagrange Error Bound Topic 10.12

If $|f^{(n+1)}(t)| \le M$ for every $t$ between $c$ and $x$, then $$\left|f(x) - P_n(x)\right| \le \frac{M}{(n+1)!}\,|x - c|^{n+1}.$$ $M$ must bound the $(n+1)$st derivative on the whole interval between $c$ and $x$ — not only at the centre.

Worked Example: $f(x) = e^{2x}$, $P_2(x) = 1 + 2x + 2x^2$, approximate $f(0.1)$. $f'''(x) = 8e^{2x}$ is increasing, so on $[0, 0.1]$ the maximum is $M = 8e^{0.2}$.
Bound $= \frac{8e^{0.2}}{3!}(0.1)^3 \approx 0.00163$. The actual error is $e^{0.2} - 1.22 \approx 0.00140$, below the bound. Note that $M = 8$ (the value at $x = 0$) would give $0.00133$, which is smaller than the actual error — an invalid bound.
Common Traps: (1) Using the $n$th derivative, $n!$ or $|x - c|^n$ instead of the $(n+1)$st. (2) Evaluating $M$ only at the centre. (3) Writing "$\le$ bound" for the error but then stating $f(x)$ equals $P_n(x)$. The conclusion is an interval: $P_n(x) - \text{bound} \le f(x) \le P_n(x) + \text{bound}$.
10.13 Radius and Interval of Convergence of Power Series Topic 10.13

A power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (x - a)^n$ converges on an interval centred at $a$. Its radius of convergence $R$ is found with the Ratio Test: the series converges absolutely when $\lim \left|\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\right| |x - a| \lt 1$. The interval of convergence is $(a - R, a + R)$ plus whichever endpoints give convergent series — each endpoint must be tested separately.

Worked Example: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(x - 3)^n}{n \cdot 4^n}$. Ratio: $\frac{n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{|x - 3|}{4} \to \frac{|x - 3|}{4} \lt 1$ ⟹ $R = 4$, open interval $(-1, 7)$.
• $x = 7$: $\sum \frac{4^n}{n\,4^n} = \sum \frac{1}{n}$, harmonic, diverges.
• $x = -1$: $\sum \frac{(-4)^n}{n\,4^n} = \sum \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$, converges by the AST.
Interval of convergence: $[-1, 7)$.
Common Traps: (1) Reporting $R = \frac{1}{4}$ (inverting the ratio). (2) Stopping at the open interval and never testing the endpoints. (3) Using the Ratio Test at an endpoint — it always gives $L = 1$ there. (4) For $\sum c^n (x - a)^n$, the radius is $\frac{1}{c}$, not $c$.
10.14 Finding Taylor or Maclaurin Series for a Function Topic 10.14

A Taylor series is the Taylor polynomial with infinitely many terms, $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(c)}{n!}(x - c)^n$. The four Maclaurin series named in the CED: $$e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}, \qquad \sin x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}, \qquad \cos x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!} \quad (\text{all } x),$$ $$\frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n \quad (|x| \lt 1).$$

Substitution: replace $x$ by an expression. $e^{-x^2} = \sum \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{n!}$, so $x e^{-x^2} = x - x^3 + \frac{x^5}{2} - \frac{x^7}{6} + \cdots$.
Reading derivatives from a series: for $e^{x^2} = \sum \frac{x^{2n}}{n!}$, the coefficient of $x^6$ is $\frac{1}{3!} = \frac{1}{6}$, so $f^{(6)}(0) = 6! \cdot \frac{1}{6} = 120$.
Common Traps: (1) Reporting the coefficient when the question asks for the derivative (or vice versa). (2) Losing the alternating sign after substituting $-x^2$. (3) Matching the wrong index: the $x^6$ term of $e^{x^2}$ comes from $n = 3$, not $n = 6$.
10.15 Representing Functions as Power Series Topic 10.15

Inside its interval of convergence, a power series may be differentiated and integrated term by term; the radius stays the same, but endpoint behaviour can change.

Worked Examples:
• $\frac{x}{1 - x^3} = x\sum (x^3)^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^{3n+1}$ for $|x| \lt 1$.
• Differentiate $\frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum x^n$: $\frac{1}{(1 - x)^2} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n x^{n-1}$, so $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n x^n = \frac{x}{(1 - x)^2}$; at $x = \frac{1}{2}$, $\sum \frac{n}{2^n} = 2$.
• Integrate $\frac{1}{1 + t} = \sum (-1)^n t^n$ from $0$ to $x$: $\ln(1 + x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n}$, so $\sum \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n\,2^n} = \ln\frac{3}{2}$.
• Integrate $\frac{1}{1 + t^2}$: $\arctan x = x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^5}{5} - \cdots$.
Common Traps: (1) Forgetting the extra factor of $x$ when converting $\sum n x^{n-1}$ to $\sum n x^n$. (2) Dropping the constant of integration — fix it with a known value such as $\ln(1 + 0) = 0$. (3) Assuming the endpoints of the new series behave like those of the original.
Unit 10 Strategy Map: Which Convergence Test First? Topics 10.3–10.9
1. Do the terms approach $0$? If not ⟹ diverges (nth term test, 10.3).
2. Is it geometric ($a r^n$) or a p-series ($\frac{1}{n^p}$)? ⟹ decide immediately (10.2, 10.5).
3. Factorials or $c^n$? ⟹ Ratio Test (10.8).
4. A rational or algebraic expression in $n$? ⟹ Limit Comparison with $\frac{1}{n^p}$ from the dominant powers (10.6).
5. Alternating signs? ⟹ test $\sum |a_n|$ first; if that diverges, use the AST and call it conditionally convergent (10.7, 10.9).
6. A term $f(n)$ that is easy to integrate, such as $\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}$? ⟹ Integral Test (10.4).

Model FRQ A (AP-style original, Topics 10.11–10.12): a function $f$ has derivatives of all orders, with $f(1) = 4$, $f'(1) = -2$, $f''(1) = 6$, $f'''(1) = -12$, and $|f^{(4)}(x)| \le 48$ for $1 \le x \le 1.2$.
(a) Write $P_3(x)$ about $x = 1$: $P_3(x) = 4 - 2(x - 1) + \frac{6}{2!}(x - 1)^2 - \frac{12}{3!}(x - 1)^3 = 4 - 2(x - 1) + 3(x - 1)^2 - 2(x - 1)^3$.
(b) Approximate $f(1.2)$: $P_3(1.2) = 4 - 0.4 + 0.12 - 0.016 = 3.704$.
(c) Lagrange bound: $|f(1.2) - P_3(1.2)| \le \frac{48}{4!}(0.2)^4 = 0.0032$.
(d) Conclusion: $3.7008 \le f(1.2) \le 3.7072$.
Typical scoring: one point for the two lower-order terms, one for the two higher-order terms (factorials included), one for the approximation, one for the form of the Lagrange bound, one for the numerical bound.
Model FRQ B (AP-style original, Topics 10.9, 10.10, 10.13): $f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n (x - 2)^n}{\sqrt{n}\,3^n}$.
(a) Ratio Test: $\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n+1}}\cdot\frac{|x - 2|}{3} \to \frac{|x - 2|}{3} \lt 1$ ⟹ $R = 3$.
(b) Endpoints: at $x = 5$ the series is $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}}$, which converges by the AST ($\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ positive, decreasing, limit $0$); at $x = -1$ it is $\sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$, a p-series with $p = \frac{1}{2} \le 1$, which diverges. Interval of convergence: $(-1, 5]$.
(c) At $x = 5$: $\sum \left|\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}}\right| = \sum \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ diverges, so the convergence is conditional.
(d) $f(3) = \sum \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}\,3^n}$ satisfies the AST, so $|f(3) - S_3| \le b_4 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4}\cdot 3^4} = \frac{1}{162}$.
Typical scoring: one point for setting up the ratio, one for the limit / radius, one for each endpoint with the test named, one for the interval, one for the error bound with the first omitted term.
Language Note: Name every test and every hypothesis in full sentences: "By the Alternating Series Test, since $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ is positive, decreasing, and $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} = 0$, the series converges." Say "the series converges conditionally," "the error is at most," and "the Ratio Test is inconclusive." Never write "the series converges to $0$" when you mean "the terms approach $0$."
