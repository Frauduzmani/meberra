    SECTION 1 • 10 QUESTIONS • 30 MIN

    Section 1 — Reading and comprehension

    The official syllabus describes B2-level (upper intermediate) English texts — non-fiction, literary or journalistic — and tests comprehension, abstraction, the relationships between parts of a text, and implied meaning. No specialist knowledge is required: every answer must be justified by the text itself. With 3 minutes per question, you have time to read carefully — but not to re-read the whole passage for every item.

    The seven question typesKnow what is being asked
    TypeTypical stemWhat to do
    Main idea"best expresses the main point"Find the author's conclusion (often after Rather, However, In short).
    Detail"According to the passage..."Locate the key word in the text; the answer is a paraphrase of that sentence.
    Inference"It can be inferred..."Choose what must follow from the text, not what might be true.
    Vocabulary in context"most nearly means"Substitute each option into the sentence; the dictionary's first meaning is often a trap.
    Purpose / function"mentions X in order to"Ask which claim the example supports — look at the next sentence.
    Reference"'this' refers to"Go back to the previous sentence and find the matching noun or verb.
    Tone / attitude"the tone is best described as"Collect evaluative adjectives and adverbs; balanced language = cautious tone.
    Worked exampleEliminate, then confirm

    "Electric scooters were welcomed as a clean alternative to short car trips. Surveys, however, show that most scooter journeys replace walking or public transport, not driving. Their environmental benefit is therefore smaller than early advocates claimed."

    Question: Which statement would the author most likely agree with?

    (1) "E-scooters have no environmental benefit." — Too extreme: the text says "smaller", not "none".
    (2) "E-scooters mostly replace car trips." — Contradicted: surveys show the opposite.
    (3) "Early advocates overestimated the benefit of e-scooters." — Supported: "smaller than early advocates claimed".
    (4) "Public transport should be replaced by e-scooters." — Not stated: no recommendation is made.
    Answer: statement (3).
    Typical trapsWhy wrong answers look right
    1. Extreme words: always, never, only, all, no, prove — rarely justified by a cautious academic text.
    2. True but not stated: an option that matches general knowledge but not the passage is wrong.
    3. Old view vs new view: texts often present a belief and then correct it; check which one the question asks about.
    4. Right words, wrong relationship: an option can reuse words from the text but connect them incorrectly (wrong subject, wrong cause).
    5. Reversed question: watch for NOT, EXCEPT, least, smallest in the stem.

    Section 2 — Mathematics

    Syllabus: numbers (operations, divisibility, powers, roots, percentages), algebra (expressions, equations, inequalities, factorisation, systems), geometry (plane and solid figures, similarity, Cartesian coordinates, lines and circles), functions (composition, inverses, graph transformations, elementary functions), exponentials and logarithms, combinatorics and probability, and basic statistics. You have 2 minutes per question and no calculator, so the numbers are designed to be handled mentally.

    Key formulasMemorise these
    Percent change: multiply by $(1 \pm p/100)$; successive changes multiply.
    Powers: $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$, $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$, $a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$
    Logs: $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$, $\log a^k = k \log a$, $\log_a b = c \iff a^c = b$
    Quadratic: $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$; $x_1 + x_2 = -\frac{b}{a}$, $x_1 x_2 = \frac{c}{a}$
    Line: $y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)$; parallel: equal $m$; perpendicular: $m_1 m_2 = -1$
    Circle: $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$
    Distance: $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$
    Counting: $n!$, $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ (order irrelevant)
    Probability: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$; independent: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$
    Statistics: sum $=$ mean $\times n$; median = middle value of the sorted list
    Worked example: inequality with a sign chartAlgebra

    Problem: Solve $\frac{x + 2}{x - 1} \gt 0$.

    1. Critical points: numerator zero at $x = -2$, denominator zero at $x = 1$.
    2. Test each interval: $x = -3$ gives $\frac{-1}{-4} \gt 0$ ✓; $x = 0$ gives $\frac{2}{-1} \lt 0$ ✗; $x = 2$ gives $\frac{4}{1} \gt 0$ ✓.
    3. Strict inequality: neither endpoint is included. Solution: $x \lt -2$ or $x \gt 1$.
    Never multiply both sides by $(x - 1)$ without knowing its sign — that is the classic error.
    Typical trapsExam-tested errors
    1. $+20\%$ then $-20\%$ is not zero change ($1.2 \times 0.8 = 0.96$).
    2. $\log(a + b) \ne \log a + \log b$; always check the domain of a log equation.
    3. $f^{-1}(x) \ne \frac{1}{f(x)}$; and $f(g(x))$ means $g$ first.
    4. "At least one" → use the complement: total minus "none".
    5. Two dice give 36 equally likely ordered outcomes, not 11 equally likely sums.

    SECTION 3 • 15 QUESTIONS • 30 MIN

    Section 3 — Reasoning on texts and data

    Syllabus: logic (connectives, quantifiers, negation, counterexamples, necessary and sufficient conditions), interpreting data (extracting information from texts, tables and graphs, drawing conclusions, sorting criteria) and problem solving (translating a situation into a model, estimation, percentages, ratios, proportionality, and checking whether a result makes sense).

    Logic essentialsConnectives & quantifiers
    Negation of "all": "every $x$ is $P$" → "at least one $x$ is not $P$"
    Negation of "some": "some $x$ is $P$" → "no $x$ is $P$"
    De Morgan: not ($A$ and $B$) = (not $A$) or (not $B$)
    Conditional: "if $P$ then $Q$" ≡ "if not $Q$ then not $P$" (contrapositive)
    Not equivalent: converse $Q \Rightarrow P$ and inverse $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$
    Conditions: in $P \Rightarrow Q$, $P$ is sufficient for $Q$ and $Q$ is necessary for $P$
    "$P$ only if $Q$": means $P \Rightarrow Q$
    Counterexample: one case with $P$ true and $Q$ false disproves "if $P$ then $Q$"
    Worked example: weighted average from a tableData interpretation

    Problem: Class X (20 students) has a mean score of 70; class Y (30 students) has a mean of 80. What is the mean of all 50 students?

    1. Do not average the averages: $\frac{70 + 80}{2} = 75$ ignores class sizes.
    2. Total points: $20 \cdot 70 + 30 \cdot 80 = 1400 + 2400 = 3800$.
    3. Mean: $\frac{3800}{50} = 76$.
    Sense check: the answer is closer to 80 because class Y is larger.
    Typical trapsRead the rule, not your intuition
    1. Confusing a rate (percentage) with a count in tables.
    2. Percentage change uses the original value as the denominator.
    3. "And" requires both conditions; "or" requires at least one.
    4. Inverse proportionality: more workers → less time.
    5. After a long calculation, check the order of magnitude: is the answer plausible?

    SECTION 4 • 10 QUESTIONS • 20 MIN

    Section 4 — Biology

    Syllabus: biological molecules (water, carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids); cell biology (prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, organelles, plant-specific structures); cell cycle, division and inheritance (DNA and RNA, replication, transcription, translation, mitosis, meiosis, Mendelian genetics); plant biology and ecology (photosynthesis, energy flow, food chains, biotic interactions); animal anatomy and physiology (tissues, musculoskeletal, digestive, respiratory, circulatory, excretory and nervous systems). Note: Politecnico di Milano does not count this section in its CEnT-S conversion, but other universities may.

    Core factsHigh-yield summary
    DNA: sugar–phosphate backbone (phosphodiester bonds); bases paired by hydrogen bonds: A–T (2), G–C (3); strands antiparallel.
    Chargaff: in double-stranded DNA, $\%A = \%T$ and $\%G = \%C$.
    Central dogma: DNA → (transcription, nucleus) → mRNA → (translation, ribosome) → protein. RNA uses U instead of T.
    Prokaryotes: no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles; they do have ribosomes (70S), a cell membrane and DNA.
    Mitosis: $2n \to 2n$, two identical cells. Meiosis: $2n \to n$, four genetically different cells.
    Monohybrid cross $Aa \times Aa$: genotypes $1:2:1$, phenotypes $3:1$.
    Photosynthesis: $6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$; $\text{O}_2$ comes from water.
    Energy flow: roughly $10\%$ passes to each next trophic level.
    Vessels: arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins towards it (the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood).
    Nephron: filtration (glomerulus) → reabsorption (proximal tubule) → loop of Henle → collecting duct.
    Worked example: test crossMendelian genetics

    Problem: A plant with the dominant phenotype is crossed with a homozygous recessive plant ($aa$). Half of the offspring show the recessive phenotype. What was the genotype of the first plant?

    1. If the plant were $AA$: $AA \times aa$ → all $Aa$ → 100% dominant. ✗
    2. If it were $Aa$: $Aa \times aa$ → $\frac{1}{2} Aa$, $\frac{1}{2} aa$ → 50% recessive. ✓
    Genotype: $Aa$. This is why a cross with $aa$ is called a test cross: it reveals hidden recessive alleles.
    Typical trapsPrecise vocabulary
    1. Chromosomes vs chromatids: after S phase the chromosome number stays $2n$; only the chromatid count doubles.
    2. mRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the template strand.
    3. "Among offspring with the dominant phenotype..." is a conditional fraction (denominator 3, not 4).
    4. Artery/vein names depend on direction, not on oxygen content.

    Section 5 — Chemistry

    Syllabus: macroscopic properties (states of matter, mixtures and separation, basic gas laws), microscopic properties (atomic structure, Lewis structures, bonding, intermolecular forces, molecular geometry), the periodic table and periodic trends, reactions and stoichiometry (balancing, moles, Avogadro's constant, limiting reactant, concentration), thermodynamics, kinetics and equilibrium, compounds and solutions, acids and bases (pH, indicators, neutralisation, buffers), redox, organic chemistry (hybridisation, isomerism, functional groups) and applied chemistry. Politecnico di Milano does not count this section in its conversion.

    Key relationshipsNo calculator — keep numbers simple
    Moles: $n = \frac{m}{M}$; particles $= n N_A$, $N_A \approx 6.0 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$
    Concentration: $c = \frac{n}{V}$; dilution $c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2$
    Ideal gas: $pV = nRT$ with $T$ in kelvin ($T = \theta + 273$)
    pH: $\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]$; at $25^\circ$C, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14$
    Oxidation numbers: sum $= 0$ (neutral) or the ion charge; O $= -2$, H $= +1$, group 1 $= +1$
    VSEPR: 4 regions: 4 bonds tetrahedral, 3 + 1 lone pair trigonal pyramidal, 2 + 2 bent
    Periodic trends: radius ↑ down a group, ↓ across a period; electronegativity the opposite
    Le Chatelier: the system shifts to oppose a change; a catalyst does not shift equilibrium
    Worked example: neutralisationStoichiometry + acids

    Problem: What volume of $0.10$ mol/L NaOH neutralises $20$ mL of $0.20$ mol/L $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$?

    1. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is diprotic: $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
    2. Moles of acid: $0.20 \times 0.020 = 0.0040$ mol ⟹ moles of NaOH needed: $0.0080$ mol.
    3. Volume: $\frac{0.0080}{0.10} = 0.080$ L $= 80$ mL.
    Forgetting the factor 2 gives 40 mL — the most common wrong answer.
    Typical trapsUnits and definitions
    1. Gas laws in °C instead of K.
    2. Molecules vs atoms: one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecule contains 3 atoms.
    3. Electron geometry vs molecular shape (lone pairs are not "seen" in the shape name).
    4. In dilution, use the final volume, not the volume of water added.

    SECTION 6 • 5 QUESTIONS • 10 MIN

    Section 6 — Physics

    Syllabus: physical quantities (SI units, scientific notation, vectors, graphs), kinematics (uniform and accelerated motion, free fall, circular motion, relative motion), dynamics, work and energy (Newton's laws, common forces, work, power, energy conservation), fluids and thermodynamics (Pascal, Stevin and Archimedes, Bernoulli, ideal gas law, temperature scales, qualitative first and second laws) and electromagnetism and waves (Coulomb's law, electric field, Ohm's law, magnetic fields, waves and the electromagnetic spectrum). Only 5 questions in 10 minutes — 2 minutes each, so recall formulas instantly.

    Formula sheet (not provided in the exam)Take $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$ unless told otherwise
    Kinematics: $v = v_0 + at$, $s = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$, $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2as$
    Circular motion: $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$, $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$
    Newton: $\sum F = ma$; weight $mg$; friction $f = \mu N$
    Energy: $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, $E_p = mgh$, $W = Fs\cos\theta$, $P = \frac{W}{t} = Fv$
    Fluids: $p = p_0 + \rho g h$; buoyancy $F_B = \rho_{fluid} V g$; $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$
    Ideal gas: $pV = nRT$; $T(\text{K}) = \theta(^\circ\text{C}) + 273$
    Electricity: $V = IR$, $P = VI$; series $R = R_1 + R_2$; parallel $\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$
    Coulomb: $F = k\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$; field $E = \frac{F}{q}$
    Waves: $v = \lambda f$, $T = \frac{1}{f}$; light $c = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s
    Units: $1\ \text{km/h} = \frac{1}{3.6}\ \text{m/s}$; $1\ \text{kPa} = 10^3$ Pa
    Worked example: Archimedes' principleFluids

    Problem: A solid block of volume $2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$ and mass $1.5$ kg is held fully under water ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$). When released, does it rise or sink, and what is the net force?

    1. Buoyant force: $F_B = \rho V g = 1000 \cdot 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \cdot 10 = 20$ N.
    2. Weight: $mg = 1.5 \cdot 10 = 15$ N.
    3. Net force: $20 - 15 = 5$ N upwards ⟹ the block rises.
    Shortcut: block density $= \frac{1.5}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 750\ \text{kg/m}^3 \lt 1000$ ⟹ it floats.
    Typical trapsCheck units first
    1. Forgetting the $\frac{1}{2}$ in $s = \frac{1}{2}at^2$ and $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$.
    2. Using mass (kg) where a force (N) is needed: weight $= mg$.
    3. Absolute vs gauge pressure: "how much greater than at the surface" means $\rho g h$ only.
    4. Inverse-square laws: tripling the distance divides the force by 9.
    5. km/h → m/s: divide by 3.6 (the m/s value is always smaller).
