ACADEMIC LECTURE NOTES & THEORY DEEP-DIVE

AP Physics Theoretical Foundations & Engineering Mechanics

Formal physical laws, vector calculus derivations, free-body analysis, and qualitative causal reasoning required for College Board 5/5 mastery and PoliMi / RWTH Aachen engineering admissions:

Module 1: Classical Dynamics, Force Vectors & Constraint Equations Unit 1 & 2 • Force Architecture

Newton's Second Law in Vector Form: The acceleration of a system is directly proportional to the vector sum of all external forces acting upon it and inversely proportional to its mass:
∑ \vec{F}_{ext} = m · \vec{a} = d\vec{p}/dt

Free-Body Diagram (FBD) Rules for Full AP Credit:
1. Represent the body as a distinct point mass or extended rigid object.
2. Draw force vectors starting exactly at their point of application (e.g., gravity starts at the Center of Mass; normal force starts at the contact surface).
3. Never draw component vectors (such as $mg \sin\theta$ or $mg \cos\theta$) on the official FBD! Draw only the primary physical forces ($mg$, $F_N$, $F_f$, $T$). Components belong only in your scratch working.
Static vs. Kinetic Friction: Static friction is an inequality: $f_s \le \mu_s F_N$. It only reaches its maximum value $\mu_s F_N$ at the verge of slipping (*impending motion*). Once slipping begins, kinetic friction governs: $f_k = \mu_k F_N$.
Module 2: Work-Energy Theorem, Conservative Fields & Momentum Unit 3 & 4 • Conservation Principles

Work Done by a Variable Force: Work is the line integral of force along a displacement path:
W = ∫_{r_1}^{r_2} \vec{F} · d\vec{r} = ∫ F \cos(\theta) dr
The Work-Energy Theorem: The net work done by all forces (conservative and non-conservative) equals the change in kinetic energy: W_{net} = ∆K = ½ m v_f² - ½ m v_i².

Conservative Forces & Potential Energy Gradient: For any conservative force field, the force is the negative spatial derivative (gradient) of the potential energy function:
F_x = - dU/dx \implies ∆U = - ∫_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x dx
Equilibrium Points: $dU/dx = 0$. If $d^2U/dx^2 > 0$, equilibrium is stable (concave up); if $d^2U/dx^2 < 0$, it is unstable.

Linear Momentum & Impulse: \vec{J} = ∫ \vec{F}_{net} dt = ∆\vec{p}. In any isolated system ($\sum \vec{F}_{ext} = 0$), total momentum is strictly conserved in all collisions (elastic, inelastic, completely inelastic). Kinetic energy is conserved only in perfectly elastic collisions!

Module 3: Rotational Dynamics & Angular Momentum Conservation Unit 5 & 6 • Rotational Mechanics

Rotational Analogue of Newton's 2nd Law:
∑ \vec{τ}_{net} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = I · \vec{α} = d\vec{L}/dt
Where the rotational inertia about an axis is given by: I = ∫ r² dm (Solid cylinder: $½ M R²$; Solid sphere: $\frac{2}{5} M R²$; Rod about center: $\frac{1}{12} M L²$).

Rolling Without Slipping Condition: A rigid body of radius $R$ rolling without slipping couples linear and rotational kinematics:
v_{cm} = R · ω \quad \text{and} \quad a_{cm} = R · α
Total kinetic energy: K_{total} = K_{trans} + K_{rot} = ½ M v_{cm}² + ½ I_{cm} ω².
Conservation of Angular Momentum: If external torque $\sum \vec{\tau}_{ext} = 0$, then $I_i \omega_i = I_f \omega_f$. Note that a particle moving along a straight line with linear momentum $p$ also possesses angular momentum about an arbitrary origin: $L = r_\perp \cdot p = m v r \sin(\theta)$!
Module 4: Electromagnetism & Field Theory (Physics C Scope) Physics C • Maxwellian Foundations

Gauss's Law for Electrostatics: The total electric flux through any closed Gaussian surface equals the enclosed charge divided by the permittivity of free space:
\oint \vec{E} · d\vec{A} = Q_{enclosed} / ε_0

Faraday's Law & Lenz's Law: An induced electromotive force (EMF) is generated in any loop by a time-varying magnetic flux $\Phi_B = \int \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A}$:
\mathcal{E} = - d\Phi_B / dt = - d/dt [ ∫ \vec{B} · d\vec{A} ]
Lenz's Law (The Negative Sign): The direction of any magnetically induced current is such that the magnetic field created by the induced current opposes the initial change in magnetic flux that produced it.

MODEL 1: COLLEGE BOARD AP PHYSICS 1 / C FRQ Dönme Dinamiği & Enerji

SORU (Rotational Dynamics): Kütlesi M ve yarıçapı R olan homojen bir diskin (I = ½ M R²) etrafına sarılı kütlesiz ipin ucuna m kütleli bir blok asılmıştır. Blok serbest bırakıldığında:
(a) m kütleli blok ve M kütleli disk için serbest cisim diyagramlarını (FBD) çiziniz.
(b) Bloğun aşağı iniş çizgisel ivmesini (a) ve ipteki gerilme kuvvetini (T) M, m, g cinsinden türetiniz.
(c) Blok h kadar düştüğünde çizgisel hızını enerji korunumu yöntemiyle doğrulayınız.

Scoring Guidelines & Adım Adım Çözüm:
• (a) FBD [2 Puan]: Blok için aşağı mg, yukarı T okları. Disk için eksenden geçen tepki kuvvetleri ve teğet noktadan aşağı yönlü T gerilmesi.
• (b) Dinamik Çözüm [3 Puan]:
1. Blok için Newton 2. Yasa: mg - T = m · a => T = m(g - a).
2. Disk için Tork denklemi: τ = T · R = I · α. Kayma olmadığından α = a / R.
T · R = (½ M R²) · (a / R) => T = ½ M · a.
3. Eşitleme: m(g - a) = ½ M · a => mg = (m + ½ M) a.
İvme: a = g / (1 + M / 2m) | İp Gerilmesi: T = (m · M · g) / (2m + M).
• (c) Enerji Korunumu [2 Puan]:
Başlangıçtaki potansiyel enerji: E_i = m · g · h.
h kadar inişteki kinetik enerji: E_f = ½ m v² + ½ I ω² = ½ m v² + ½ (½ M R²) (v/R)² = ½ (m + ½ M) v².
m g h = ½ (m + ½ M) v² => v = √[ 2 m g h / (m + ½ M) ]. (Kinematik formülü v² = 2ah ile tam doğrulanır!).

UNIT 1 MASTERCLASS • AP PHYSICS 1 & C KINEMATICS

Unit 1: Kinematics (1D & 2D Motion) — Conceptual & Mathematical Rigor

Kinematics mathematically describes motion without considering the forces that cause it. Below are the foundational vector definitions, constant acceleration equations, graphical analysis techniques, and experimental design methodologies required for AP 5/5 mastery:

1.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity vs. Speed & Calculus Kinematics Topic 1.1 • Vector Kinematics

Position ($\vec{x}$ or $\vec{r}$): The location of an object relative to an origin. A vector quantity possessing both magnitude and direction.
Displacement ($\Delta \vec{x} = \vec{x}_f - \vec{x}_i$): The net vector change in position. Path-independent!
Distance ($d$): The actual scalar path length traveled ($d \ge |\Delta \vec{x}|$).

Velocity vs. Speed:
• Average Velocity: $\vec{v}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta \vec{x}}{\Delta t}$ (vector pointing in the direction of displacement).
• Average Speed: $s_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\Delta t}$ (always scalar and non-negative).
• Instantaneous Velocity (Calculus): $\vec{v}(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \vec{x}}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt}$.
• Instantaneous Speed: The absolute magnitude of instantaneous velocity: $s(t) = |\vec{v}(t)|$.
College Board Conceptual Trap: "Speeding Up" vs. "Slowing Down":
• An object is speeding up if and only if velocity and acceleration point in the SAME direction ($\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} > 0$).
• An object is slowing down if and only if velocity and acceleration point in OPPOSITE directions ($\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} < 0$).
Never assume negative acceleration means slowing down! If $v = -8$ m/s and $a = -3$ m/s², the object is moving in the $-x$ direction and speeding up!
1.2 Constant Acceleration Models: The "Big 4" Kinematics Equations Topic 1.2 • Analytical Engine

When acceleration is strictly constant ($a = \text{const}$), integration yields the four canonical kinematics equations:

The 4 Equations of Motion (Constant $a$ Only!):
1. v_f = v_0 + a·t (Velocity as a linear function of time)
2. Δx = v_0·t + (1/2)·a·t² (Displacement as a quadratic function of time)
3. v_f² = v_0² + 2·a·Δx (Timeless equation linking speeds to displacement)
4. Δx = [(v_0 + v_f) / 2] · t (Displacement as average velocity times time)

Calculus Connection (Physics C Scope): When acceleration is NOT constant ($a(t) = f(t)$), you MUST use calculus integration:
v(t) = v_0 + ∫_0^t a(t') dt' and x(t) = x_0 + ∫_0^t v(t') dt'.

1.3 Graphical Kinematics: Slopes, Areas & Physical Interpretations Topic 1.3 • Graph Interpretation

Over 35% of multiple-choice and FRQ questions require extracting physics from graphs without numbers:

Graph Derivative & Integral Matrix:
• Position vs. Time ($x-t$):
  - Slope of tangent line $= \frac{dx}{dt} = \text{Instantaneous Velocity } v(t)$.
  - Concavity: Concave up ($d^2x/dt^2 > 0$) means $a > 0$; Concave down ($d^2x/dt^2 < 0$) means $a < 0$.
• Velocity vs. Time ($v-t$):
  - Slope of tangent line $= \frac{dv}{dt} = \text{Instantaneous Acceleration } a(t)$.
  - Area under the curve: $\int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t) dt = \Delta x$ (Net Displacement).
  - Signed areas: Area above $t$-axis is $+$, area below $t$-axis is $-$.
• Acceleration vs. Time ($a-t$):
  - Area under the curve: $\int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t) dt = \Delta v = v_f - v_i$ (Change in velocity).
1.4 Two-Dimensional Motion & Projectile Independence Principle Topic 1.4 • Projectile Mechanics

The Independence Principle: Horizontal and vertical motions are strictly perpendicular and act completely independently of each other. The ONLY variable linking both dimensions is time ($t$).

Governing Projectile Equations (Launch with $v_0$ at angle $\theta$ above horizontal, $a_x = 0, a_y = -g$):
• Horizontal Dimension ($x$): Constant velocity ($a_x = 0$):
  v_x = v_0 · cos(θ) = \text{const}, \quad x(t) = (v_0 · cos(θ)) · t.
• Vertical Dimension ($y$): Free-fall acceleration ($a_y = -g$):
  v_y(t) = v_0 · sin(θ) - g·t, \quad y(t) = y_0 + (v_0 · sin(θ))·t - (1/2)·g·t².
• Apex (Highest Point): $v_y = 0 \implies t_{\text{apex}} = \frac{v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$. Maximum height: $H = \frac{(v_0 \sin\theta)^2}{2g}$.
• Horizontal Range (Flat Ground): $R = v_x \cdot (2 t_{\text{apex}}) = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$ (Maximum at $\theta = 45^\circ$).
Simultaneous Drop vs. Horizontal Launch Trap:
If Ball A is dropped from rest and Ball B is fired horizontally from the same height $h$, BOTH strike the flat ground at the exact same instant ($t = \sqrt{2h/g}$) because both have zero initial vertical velocity ($v_{y0} = 0$) and identical vertical acceleration ($-g$)!
1.5 & 1.6 Experimental Design, Graph Linearization & Written Reasoning (QQT FRQ) Topic 1.5 & 1.6 • FRQ Strategy

Graph Linearization in Kinematics Experiments:
To experimentally determine $g$ from a free-fall experiment ($h = \frac{1}{2} g t^2$):
1. Never plot $h$ vs. $t$ (yields a curved parabola).
2. Linearize by setting $y = h$ and $x = t^2$. The resulting line is $y = m x$ where slope $m = \frac{g}{2}$.
3. Calculate experimental acceleration: $g = 2 \cdot (\text{Slope of best-fit line})$.

Current FRQ Types (AP Physics 1 & C, from the May 2025 exam onward):
• Mathematical Routines (MR): symbolic and numerical derivations from first principles.
• Translation Between Representations (TBR): move between diagrams, graphs, equations, and words.
• Experimental Design and Analysis (LAB): procedure, linearization, slope interpretation, uncertainty.
• Qualitative/Quantitative Translation (QQT): a written argument that must agree with a derived equation.
(The old "Paragraph-Length Response (PLR)" label belongs to the pre-2025 exam and is no longer a separate FRQ type.)
The 3-Step Written-Reasoning Formula (QQT & TBR justifications):
1. State the fundamental physical principle: e.g., "In the absence of net external horizontal forces, horizontal velocity remains constant."
2. Connect principle to variables via mathematical proportionality: "Since $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$, flight time depends only on height $h$. Doubling $v_0$ doubles horizontal displacement $x = v_0 t$ because $t$ is unchanged."
3. Conclude with direct resolution of the prompt.

UNIT 2 MASTERCLASS • AP PHYSICS 1 & C TRANSLATIONAL DYNAMICS

Unit 2: Force and Translational Dynamics (Newton's Laws) — Mechanical Rigor

Dynamics explains the physical causes of motion through interactions between bodies and their surroundings. Below are the foundational laws of motion, free-body diagram protocols, contact force models, coupled system mechanics, and differential fluid drag formulations required for AP Physics 5/5 mastery:

2.1 Systems, Center of Mass & Free-Body Diagrams (FBD Rules) Topic 2.1 • Vector Modeling

System Definition: An object or collection of objects treated as a single entity. Forces acting between constituents of the system are internal forces and cancel out in pairs; only external forces change the momentum of the center of mass.

College Board Free-Body Diagram (FBD) Strict Protocols:
1. Dot or Body Representation: Represent the object as a point mass (or shape if torque is considered).
2. Origin of Vectors: Every force vector must originate directly on the dot or point of contact and point strictly in the direction of the exerted force.
3. No Components on Official FBDs: NEVER draw broken-down components ($mg\sin\theta, mg\cos\theta$) on an official College Board FBD! Doing so costs the diagram point. Draw only fundamental forces ($F_g, F_N, F_f, F_T$). If you need components, draw a separate scrap diagram.
4. Relative Arrow Lengths: Vector lengths must accurately reflect relative magnitudes (e.g., if moving at constant velocity on a horizontal surface with friction, $|F_{\text{pull}}| = |f_k|$).
2.2 Newton's First & Second Laws of Motion Topic 2.2 • Net Force & Acceleration

Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia): An object in an inertial reference frame remains at rest or continues at constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a non-zero net external force ($\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = 0 \iff \vec{a} = 0$).

Newton's Second Law ($\sum \vec{F} = m \vec{a}$):
The translational acceleration of a system is directly proportional to the net external force acting on it and inversely proportional to the total system inertial mass:
$$\vec{a} = \frac{\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}}}{m_{\text{total}}} \iff \sum F_x = m a_x \quad \text{and} \quad \sum F_y = m a_y$$ Key AP Principle: Net force and acceleration are always collinear and codirectional. An object moving at constant speed along a curved path is NOT in equilibrium because the direction of velocity changes ($a_c = v^2/r \ne 0$).
Apparent Weight in Non-Inertial Frames (Elevators):
A scale measures the normal contact force $F_N$ exerted by the scale on the person (apparent weight), NOT the actual gravitational force $mg$!
• Accelerating upward ($+a$): $F_N - mg = m(+a) \implies F_N = m(g + a)$ (Feels heavier).
• Accelerating downward ($-a$): $F_N - mg = m(-a) \implies F_N = m(g - a)$ (Feels lighter).
• Free-fall ($a = -g$): $F_N = m(g - g) = 0$ (Weightless sensation; no contact force).
2.3 Newton's Third Law: Action-Reaction Interaction Pairs Topic 2.3 • Interaction Pairs

Newton's Third Law: Whenever object A exerts a force on object B ($\vec{F}_{A \to B}$), object B simultaneously exerts an equal in magnitude and opposite in direction force on object A ($\vec{F}_{B \to A} = -\vec{F}_{A \to B}$).

The 4 Golden Tests for a True Newton's Third Law Pair:
1. They act on TWO DIFFERENT OBJECTS (Object A on B, Object B on A).
2. They are of the EXACT SAME PHYSICAL TYPE (both gravitational, both contact/normal, both magnetic, etc.).
3. They have IDENTICAL MAGNITUDES at all times, regardless of whether the system is accelerating, at rest, or of vastly different masses!
4. They act in OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS along the line joining the centers.
The Most Dangerous AP Misconception: $F_N$ and $F_g$ are NOT an Action-Reaction Pair!
For a book resting on a table, $F_N$ (table pushing up on book) and $F_g$ (Earth pulling down on book) both act on the same single object (the book). They balance each other due to Newton's 2nd Law equilibrium ($\sum F_y = 0$).
• The true reaction to $F_g$ (Earth on book) is the gravitational force exerted by the book pulling UP on the Earth.
• The true reaction to $F_N$ (table on book) is the normal contact force exerted by the book pushing DOWN on the table.
2.4 Contact Forces: Normal Force & Friction Dynamics (Static vs. Kinetic) Topic 2.4 • Friction Modeling

Normal Force ($F_N$): The perpendicular contact force exerted by a surface on a body preventing interpenetration. It is an electromagnetic constraint force and self-adjusts to match external normal loads.

Friction Force Laws:
• Static Friction ($f_s$): Acts when there is no relative motion between surfaces at contact. It is an inequality:
$$0 \le f_s \le f_{s,\text{max}} = \mu_s F_N$$ Static friction is self-adjusting: it equals the applied parallel shear force until the maximum threshold $\mu_s F_N$ is exceeded!
• Kinetic Friction ($f_k$): Acts when surfaces slide relative to each other. It is constant and opposes relative motion:
$$f_k = \mu_k F_N \quad (\text{where typically } \mu_k < \mu_s)$$
Static Friction on an Incline Trap:
For a crate resting stationary on an incline of angle $\theta$:
$\sum F_{\parallel} = mg\sin\theta - f_s = 0 \implies f_s = mg\sin\theta$.
Do NOT calculate $f_s = \mu_s mg\cos\theta$! The term $\mu_s mg\cos\theta$ is the maximum threshold before slipping occurs, not the actual friction force in equilibrium!
2.5 Coupled Systems, Modified Atwood Machines & Incline Analysis Topic 2.5 • System Mechanics

System Approach vs. Individual Free-Body Approach:
For connected masses (ideal massless, inextensible string over ideal frictionless pulley):
1. System Acceleration: Treat the entire multi-body setup as one system unfolding along the line of motion:
$$a = \frac{\sum F_{\text{external, driving}} - \sum F_{\text{external, resisting}}}{\sum m_{\text{total}}}$$ Internal tensions cancel out across the whole system.
2. Internal Tension ($T$): Isolate an individual mass and apply $\sum F = m_i a$.

Modified Atwood Machine (Horizontal $m_1$, Hanging $m_2$ over pulley):
• System acceleration: $a = \frac{m_2 g - f_{k1}}{m_1 + m_2}$ (where $f_{k1} = \mu_k m_1 g$).
• String Tension: Isolate hanging mass $m_2$: $m_2 g - T = m_2 a \implies T = m_2(g - a) < m_2 g$.
Critical Truth: Tension in the cord is strictly less than the weight of the hanging mass ($T < m_2 g$) because $m_2$ must have a net downward force to accelerate!
2.6 Drag Forces, Fluid Resistance & Terminal Velocity (Physics C Calculus Form) Topic 2.6 • Differential Dynamics

When a body moves through a fluid (liquid or gas), it experiences a resistive drag force $\vec{F}_{\text{drag}}$ opposite to velocity:

Linear Drag ($F_d = -bv$, slow laminar motion) & Terminal Velocity ($v_t$):
• Equation of motion: $m \frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv$.
• At terminal velocity, acceleration ceases ($a = 0$): $mg - bv_t = 0 \implies v_t = \frac{mg}{b}$.
• Exact velocity function via differential separation of variables:
$$\int_0^v \frac{dv'}{g - \frac{b}{m}v'} = \int_0^t dt' \implies v(t) = v_t \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{b}{m}t} \right)$$ • Quadratic Drag ($F_d = \frac{1}{2} C_D \rho A v^2$, high-speed turbulent flow in air):
$mg - \frac{1}{2} C_D \rho A v_t^2 = 0 \implies v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{C_D \rho A}}$.

Unit 2 (cont.): Circular Motion & Gravitation — Orbital Mechanics & Planetary Dynamics

Centripetal acceleration is the kinematic consequence of continuously redirecting the velocity vector toward a center of curvature. Below are the definitive vector derivations, free-body diagram constraints, banked curve models, critical vertical loop speeds, Newton's universal inverse-square law, Kepler's planetary laws, and orbital energy states required for AP Physics 5/5 perfection and European engineering programs (PoliMi, TUM, KIT):

2.9 (a) Centripetal Acceleration & The "Centripetal Force" Misconception CED Topic 2.9 • Radial Kinematics

Kinematics of Uniform Circular Motion (UCM): When a particle travels in a circular path of radius $r$ at constant speed $v$, its velocity magnitude is constant, but its direction changes continuously. This generates a purely radial, inward-directed acceleration: $$\vec{a}_c = -\frac{v^2}{r} \hat{r} = -\omega^2 r \hat{r} = -\frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} \hat{r}$$ where $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$ is the angular speed and $T$ is the period.

Non-Uniform Circular Motion (Tangential Acceleration):
If speed changes along the curve, the particle possesses both radial and tangential accelerations: $$\vec{a}_{\text{total}} = \vec{a}_c + \vec{a}_t = -\left(\frac{v^2}{r}\right)\hat{r} + \left(\frac{dv}{dt}\right)\hat{\theta} \implies |\vec{a}_{\text{total}}| = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2}$$
College Board Strict FBD Mandate: "Centripetal Force" is NOT a Physical Force!
There is NO distinct physical interaction called "centripetal force" ($F_c$), and centrifugal force does NOT exist in an inertial reference frame!
• The Core Truth: $\sum F_r = m a_c = \frac{m v^2}{r}$ is Newton's Second Law applied along the radial axis. The required centripetal acceleration is provided by real physical forces (tension $F_T$, gravity $F_g$, normal force $F_N$, or static friction $f_s$).
• Official Rubric Penalty: If you draw a vector labeled "$F_c$" on an AP Free-Body Diagram, College Board graders automatically deduct the diagram point! Draw ONLY the actual physical agents ($F_T, F_g, F_N, f_s$).
2.9 (b) Banked Turns, Conical Pendulums & Vertical Loop Dynamics CED Topic 2.9 • Curved Dynamics

Frictionless Banked Curve & Conical Pendulum Equilibrium:
A road banked at angle $\theta$ allows a vehicle of mass $m$ to negotiate a curve of radius $r$ at a specific design speed $v_{\text{design}}$ with zero lateral friction: $$\text{Vertical: } F_N \cos\theta - mg = 0 \implies F_N \cos\theta = mg$$ $$\text{Horizontal (Radial): } F_N \sin\theta = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$ Dividing the radial equation by the vertical equation eliminates mass and normal force: $$\tan\theta = \frac{v_{\text{design}}^2}{r g} \implies v_{\text{design}} = \sqrt{r g \tan\theta}$$ • Speed Variations with Friction: If $v > v_{\text{design}}$, the car tends to skid up the incline, requiring static friction directed down the bank. If $v < v_{\text{design}}$, static friction acts up the bank.

Vertical Circular Loop & The Critical Threshold Speed ($v_{\text{crit}}$):
For an object of mass $m$ moving in a vertical circle of radius $R$:
1. At the Bottom: Radial net force points upward: $$\sum F_r = F_N - mg = \frac{m v_{\text{bot}}^2}{R} \implies F_N = mg + \frac{m v_{\text{bot}}^2}{R} \quad (\text{Maximum Apparent Weight!})$$ 2. At the Top: Both normal force and gravity point downward toward the center: $$\sum F_r = F_N + mg = \frac{m v_{\text{top}}^2}{R} \implies F_N = \frac{m v_{\text{top}}^2}{R} - mg$$ 3. Critical Speed at the Apex: To complete the loop without falling or losing contact, the track must exert at least an infinitesimal contact force ($F_N \ge 0$). At the threshold where $F_N \to 0$: $$mg = \frac{m v_{\text{crit}}^2}{R} \implies v_{\text{crit}} = \sqrt{g R}$$ 4. Minimum Release Height for a Frictionless Roller Coaster: By conservation of mechanical energy ($E_{\text{release}} = E_{\text{top}}$): $$m g h = m g (2R) + \frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{top}}^2 \implies g h = 2g R + \frac{1}{2}(g R) \implies h_{\text{min}} = \frac{5}{2}R = 2.5R$$
2.6 Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation & Local Gravitational Field Strength CED Topic 2.6 • Universal Gravitation

The Universal Inverse-Square Law: Every point mass attracts every other point mass with a force along the line connecting their centers: $$F_g = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \quad \left(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \frac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{kg}^2}\right)$$ By Newton's Third Law, both masses experience forces of identical magnitude regardless of any difference in their masses ($\vec{F}_{1 \to 2} = -\vec{F}_{2 \to 1}$).

Gravitational Field Strength ($g$): The gravitational force per unit mass exerted on a test mass: $$g(r) = \frac{F_g}{m} = \frac{G M}{r^2}$$ • At Planet Surface ($r = R_p$): $g_0 = \frac{G M}{R_p^2}$.
• Altitude $h$ above Surface ($r = R_p + h$): $g(h) = \frac{G M}{(R_p + h)^2} = g_0 \left(\frac{R_p}{R_p + h}\right)^2$.
• Interior Field for a Uniform Density Planet ($r < R_p$): By Newton's Shell Theorem, outer shells exert zero net gravity; only the enclosed mass ($M_{\text{enc}} = M \frac{r^3}{R_p^3}$) acts: $$g_{\text{interior}}(r) = \frac{G M_{\text{enc}}}{r^2} = \frac{G M r}{R_p^3} = g_0 \left(\frac{r}{R_p}\right) \quad (\text{Linear decrease to zero at the core!})$$
2.9 (c) Orbital Mechanics, Kepler's Laws & Gravitational Energy Binding States CED 2.9 + Units 3 & 6 • Celestial Mechanics

Circular Satellite Orbits: Gravity acts as the sole centripetal force holding satellite of mass $m$ in orbit around planet of mass $M$: $$\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v_{\text{orb}}^2}{r} \implies v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}$$ Key AP Takeaway: Orbital speed is completely independent of the satellite's mass $m$, and decreases inversely with the square root of the orbital radius ($v_{\text{orb}} \propto 1/\sqrt{r}$).

Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion:
1. Kepler's 1st Law (Elliptical Orbits): Planetary orbits are ellipses with the central mass located at one focus.
2. Kepler's 2nd Law (Equal Areas in Equal Times): A line segment joining planet and star sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time ($dA/dt = \text{const}$).
  • Conservation of Angular Momentum: Gravitational force is strictly a central force ($\vec{r} \parallel \vec{F}_g$), so the net gravitational torque is zero: $$\vec{\tau}_{\text{net}} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}_g = 0 \implies \vec{L} = m (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \text{constant}$$ $$\implies m r_p v_p = m r_a v_a \implies v_p r_p = v_a r_a$$ The planet moves fastest at perihelion ($r_p$, closest) and slowest at aphelion ($r_a$, farthest).
3. Kepler's 3rd Law (Harmonic Law Derivation): Using $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$ in the circular orbital equation: $$\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{G M}{r} \implies T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{G M}\right) r^3 \iff \frac{T^2}{r^3} = \text{constant}$$
Orbital Energy & Escape Speed Formulation:
• Universal Gravitational Potential Energy: Zero reference chosen at infinite separation ($r \to \infty$): $$U_g(r) = -\frac{G M m}{r}$$ • Total Mechanical Energy in Circular Orbit: $$E_{\text{total}} = K + U_g = \frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{orb}}^2 - \frac{G M m}{r} = \frac{G M m}{2r} - \frac{G M m}{r} = -\frac{G M m}{2r} = \frac{1}{2}U_g = -K$$ • Escape Speed ($v_{\text{esc}}$): The minimum launch speed from surface ($R_p$) to reach infinity with zero kinetic energy ($E_{\text{total}} = 0$): $$\frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{esc}}^2 - \frac{G M m}{R_p} = 0 \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{R_p}} = \sqrt{2} \cdot v_{\text{orb, surface}}$$

Unit 3: Work, Energy, and Power — The Bookkeeping of Mechanics

Energy methods replace vector bookkeeping with scalar bookkeeping. Instead of tracking every force at every instant, you choose a system, identify how energy enters or leaves it (work), and track how it is stored (kinetic, gravitational, elastic, internal). Below are the definitions, theorems, graphical tools, and written-reasoning habits that the AP Physics 1 CED (Unit 3) and Physics C: Mechanics expect:

3.1 & 3.2 (a) Translational Kinetic Energy & Work Done by a Constant Force CED Topics 3.1 & 3.2 • Energy Transfer

Translational Kinetic Energy: $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. It is a scalar, it is never negative, and it depends on the square of speed (doubling $v$ quadruples $K$). Its value depends on the reference frame.
Work: Work is the transfer of energy into or out of a system by a force acting through a displacement. For a constant force $\vec{F}$ and a straight displacement $\vec{d}$: $$W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = F d \cos\theta$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{F}$ and $\vec{d}$. Only the component of force parallel to the displacement does work. Unit: $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$.

Sign of Work (energy bookkeeping):
• $0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ$: $W > 0$ — the force transfers energy into the object.
• $\theta = 90^\circ$: $W = 0$ — e.g., the normal force on a block sliding on a level floor, or the tension in a string for an object in uniform circular motion.
• $90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ$: $W < 0$ — the force removes energy (kinetic friction on a sliding block, gravity on a rising ball).
• No displacement of the point of application $\Rightarrow$ no work, however large the force (pushing on a wall that does not move).
Everyday-Language Trap: Carrying a heavy box horizontally at constant velocity feels like "work", but the upward force of your hands is perpendicular to the displacement, so $W_{\text{hands}} = 0$. Physics work is $Fd\cos\theta$, not effort.
3.2 (b) Work Done by a Variable Force: Area Under the F–x Graph CED Topic 3.2 • Graphical & Calculus Work

When the force component along the displacement changes with position, the work is the signed area between the $F_x$–$x$ curve and the $x$-axis: $$W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x(x)\, dx$$ Area above the axis counts as positive work; area below the axis counts as negative work. In AP Physics 1 you compute areas of rectangles and triangles; in Physics C you integrate the function.

Ideal Spring (Hooke's Law, $F_s = -kx$):
• The force exerted by a person stretching a spring from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is $F = +kx$, so the work done on the spring is the area of a trapezoid: $$W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} kx\, dx = \frac{1}{2}k x_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}k x_1^2$$ • Note that this is not $\frac{1}{2}k(x_2 - x_1)^2$. Spring energy depends on the deformation measured from the natural length, not on the change in deformation.
3.2 (c) The Work–Energy Theorem CED Topic 3.2 • Net Work = ΔK

For an object that can be modelled as a point particle, the net work done by all forces equals the change in its translational kinetic energy: $$W_{\text{net}} = \sum W_i = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2$$ Derivation (constant net force): $v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2a\Delta x$ and $F_{\text{net}} = ma$ give $F_{\text{net}}\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2$.

Stopping-Distance Template (level surface, kinetic friction only):
$$-\mu_k m g\, d = 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 \implies d = \frac{v_0^2}{2\mu_k g}$$ The mass cancels, and the stopping distance scales with $v_0^2$: doubling the speed quadruples the stopping distance.
3.3 Conservative vs. Nonconservative Forces & Potential Energy CED Topic 3.3 • Stored Energy

Conservative force: the work it does on an object moving between two points is independent of the path, and the work around any closed path is zero (gravity, ideal spring force). Only conservative forces have an associated potential energy, defined by $\Delta U = -W_{\text{conservative}}$.
Nonconservative force: the work depends on the path (kinetic friction, air resistance, applied pushes). Kinetic friction around a closed path always does negative work, converting mechanical energy into internal (thermal) energy.

Potential Energy Functions (always a property of a system of interacting objects):
• Object–Earth system near the surface: $\Delta U_g = mg\Delta y$ (choose any convenient reference height $y = 0$).
• Spring–object system: $U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$, with $x$ measured from the natural (unstretched) length.
• General gravitation (two masses, zero at infinite separation): $U_G = -\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}$.
• Force from potential energy (Physics C): $F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx}$. Equilibrium where $\frac{dU}{dx} = 0$; stable at a minimum of $U$, unstable at a maximum.
System Trap: A single block does not "have" gravitational potential energy — the block–Earth system does. If you choose the block alone as the system, gravity is an external force that does work; if you choose block + Earth, you track $\Delta U_g$ instead. Never count both, or you double-count gravity.
3.4 Conservation of Energy, Mechanical Energy & Energy Bar Charts (LOL Diagrams) CED Topic 3.4 • System Bookkeeping

General energy equation for a chosen system: $$K_i + U_i + W_{\text{ext}} = K_f + U_f + \Delta E_{\text{int}}$$ If the system is closed and isolated ($W_{\text{ext}} = 0$) and no nonconservative forces act inside it ($\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$), mechanical energy is conserved: $$K_i + U_{g,i} + U_{s,i} = K_f + U_{g,f} + U_{s,f}$$ Otherwise: $\Delta E_{\text{mech}} = W_{\text{nc}}$ (for example, $W_{\text{friction}} = -f_k d$).

Energy Bar Chart (LOL Diagram) Protocol:
1. Define the system in words (e.g., "block + spring + Earth").
2. Draw the initial-state bars ($K_i$, $U_{g,i}$, $U_{s,i}$), the energy-transfer bar ($W_{\text{ext}}$, can be negative), and the final-state bars ($K_f$, $U_{g,f}$, $U_{s,f}$, $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$).
3. The sum of the bar heights on the left must equal the sum on the right — bar charts are a visual version of the energy equation.
4. A bar of zero height is drawn as an empty column, not omitted.
$K_i$ $U_{g,i}$ $U_{s,i}$ $W_{\text{ext}}$ $K_f$ $U_{g,f}$ $U_{s,f}$ $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$

Example: System = block + spring + Earth. A compressed spring launches a block up a rough ramp: 4 units of elastic energy become 1 unit of kinetic, 2 units of gravitational, and 1 unit of internal (thermal) energy. Left total = 4, right total = 4.
3.5 Power: The Rate of Energy Transfer CED Topic 3.5 • Rate of Work

Average power: $P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}$. Instantaneous power delivered by a force: $P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta$. Unit: $1\text{ W} = 1\text{ J/s}$.

Constant-Speed Engine Template: At constant velocity the net force is zero, so the driving force equals the total opposing force along the motion. On a slope with resistive force $f$: $$F_{\text{drive}} = f + mg\sin\theta \implies P = (f + mg\sin\theta)\,v$$ • A kilowatt-hour is an energy unit: $1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$.
Unit 3 Common Traps & FRQ Scoring Habits Exam Strategy • MR • TBR • LAB • QQT
Five Frequent Point-Losers:
1. Using $\frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$ for the energy change of a spring that was already stretched.
2. Writing $f_k = \mu_k mg$ on an incline (correct: $\mu_k mg\cos\theta$, or $\mu_k F_N$ in general).
3. Claiming "energy is lost". Energy is transferred out of the system or converted into internal energy — name where it goes.
4. Treating speed as path-dependent on frictionless tracks. Final speed depends only on the height drop, not on the shape of the track or the launch direction.
5. Confusing power and energy (kW vs. kWh; "watts per second" is not a unit of power).

• Mathematical Routines: start every derivation from a fundamental equation ($K_i + U_i + W = K_f + U_f + \Delta E_{\text{int}}$), then substitute; do not start from a memorised special result.

• Experimental Design and Analysis: linearize, e.g. launch height $h$ versus compression $x$ satisfies $h = \frac{k}{2mg}x^2$, so plot $h$ against $x^2$ and use slope $= \frac{k}{2mg}$.

AP Physics 1 — Unit 4: Linear Momentum — Interactions, Impulse, and Collisions

Energy tells you how much can happen; momentum tells you how objects share motion when they interact. Momentum is a vector, it is conserved for every isolated system, and it is the only reliable tool for the short, violent interactions (collisions, explosions, recoil) in which forces are unknown. Below are the definitions, theorems, graphical tools, and FRQ habits that the AP Physics 1 CED (Unit 4, Topics 4.1–4.4) and Physics C: Mechanics expect:

4.1 Linear Momentum CED Topic 4.1 • Quantity of Motion

Definition: The linear momentum of an object is the product of its mass and velocity: $$\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$$ It is a vector with the same direction as the velocity. SI unit: $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, which is identical to $\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$.
Momentum of a system: the vector sum of the momenta of its parts, $\vec{p}_{\text{sys}} = \sum m_i\vec{v}_i$. In one dimension, choose a positive direction and keep the signs; opposite motions can cancel to give zero total momentum even when every object is moving.

Momentum vs. Kinetic Energy:
• $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$ is a vector and scales linearly with speed; $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ is a scalar and scales with the square of speed.
• They are linked by $K = \frac{p^2}{2m}$, i.e. $p = \sqrt{2mK}$.
• Two objects with equal momentum magnitudes: the lighter one has more kinetic energy ($K \propto 1/m$).
• Two objects with equal kinetic energies: the heavier one has more momentum ($p \propto \sqrt{m}$).
Sign Trap: A system can have $\vec{p}_{\text{sys}} = 0$ while its total kinetic energy is large (two carts approaching each other with equal and opposite momenta). Zero momentum does not mean zero motion.
4.2 Change in Momentum and Impulse CED Topic 4.2 • Impulse–Momentum Theorem

Newton's second law in momentum form: $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}$. The net force is the rate of change of momentum — the slope of a $p$–$t$ graph.
Impulse: the impulse delivered by a force over a time interval is $$\vec{J} = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \vec{F}\,dt = \vec{F}_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t$$ Impulse–Momentum Theorem: the net impulse on an object equals its change in momentum: $$\vec{J}_{\text{net}} = \Delta\vec{p} = m\vec{v}_f - m\vec{v}_i$$

Graphical Tools (heavily tested in MCQ and TBR FRQs):
• Area under a force–time graph = impulse = $\Delta p$. Area above the $t$-axis is positive; area below is negative.
• Slope of a momentum–time graph = net force.
• Example: a bat–ball force rises linearly from $0$ to $600\text{ N}$ and back to $0$ over $0.010\text{ s}$. Impulse = triangle area $= \frac{1}{2}(0.010)(600) = 3.0\text{ N}\cdot\text{s}$. A $0.15\text{ kg}$ ball at rest leaves at $3.0/0.15 = 20\text{ m/s}$.
Graph Slope means Area means
$F$ vs. $t$ (not used) Impulse $J = \Delta p$
$p$ vs. $t$ Net force $F_{\text{net}}$ (not used)
$F$ vs. $x$ (Unit 3) (not used) Work $W = \Delta K$
The Time–Force Trade-Off: For a fixed $\Delta p$, $F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$. Airbags, helmets, crumple zones, and bending your knees on landing all increase $\Delta t$ and therefore reduce the average force. They do not reduce the change in momentum.
Rebound Trap: A ball that bounces back experiences a larger impulse than one that stops. With $v_i = +10\text{ m/s}$ and $v_f = -8.0\text{ m/s}$, $\Delta v = -18\text{ m/s}$, not $-2\text{ m/s}$. Always write velocities with signs before subtracting.
4.3 Conservation of Linear Momentum: System Selection & Center of Mass CED Topic 4.3 • Isolated Systems

Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum: if the net external force on a system is zero, the total momentum of the system is constant: $$\sum\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = 0 \implies \vec{p}_{\text{sys},i} = \vec{p}_{\text{sys},f}$$ Internal forces (between parts of the system) come in Newton's-third-law pairs, deliver equal and opposite impulses, and cancel. They can transfer momentum between parts of the system but cannot change the total.

System Selection (the first point on many FRQs):
• Include every object that exerts a significant force during the interaction. For a collision of two carts, the system is "cart A + cart B".
• For a ball falling to the ground, the ball alone is not isolated (gravity is external); the ball–Earth system is.
• Approximately isolated: during a very short collision, external forces such as friction or gravity deliver negligible impulse compared with the large internal forces, so momentum is conserved "just before" to "just after".
• Component conservation: if external forces act only vertically (e.g., gravity and normal force), horizontal momentum is still conserved.
Center of Mass Velocity: $$\vec{v}_{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec{v}_i}{\sum m_i} = \frac{\vec{p}_{\text{sys}}}{M}, \qquad \sum\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = M\vec{a}_{cm}$$ • If the system is isolated, $\vec{v}_{cm}$ is constant — before, during, and after any collision, of any type.
• A shell that explodes in mid-air: its center of mass continues along the original parabola, because the explosion forces are internal and the only external force is gravity.
Two-Dimensional Momentum: momentum is conserved separately in each direction: $$\sum p_{x,i} = \sum p_{x,f}, \qquad \sum p_{y,i} = \sum p_{y,f}$$ Example: a $2.0\text{ kg}$ puck moving east at $3.0\text{ m/s}$ sticks to a $1.0\text{ kg}$ puck moving north at $4.0\text{ m/s}$. Then $p_x = 6.0$, $p_y = 4.0\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$; $v_f = \frac{\sqrt{6.0^2 + 4.0^2}}{3.0} \approx 2.4\text{ m/s}$ at $\tan^{-1}(4.0/6.0) \approx 34^\circ$ north of east.
Recoil Trap: Action–reaction pairs give equal and opposite forces and impulses, not equal speeds. A $60\text{ kg}$ skater who throws a $3.0\text{ kg}$ ball at $8.0\text{ m/s}$ recoils at only $\frac{3.0 \times 8.0}{60} = 0.40\text{ m/s}$.
4.4 Elastic and Inelastic Collisions: The Kinetic Energy Check CED Topic 4.4 • Collision Classification

Momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system. What distinguishes collision types is what happens to the total kinetic energy of the system:

Classification Algorithm:
1. Confirm $\vec{p}_i = \vec{p}_f$ (if not, the system is not isolated or the data are wrong).
2. Compute $K_i = \sum\frac{1}{2}m v_i^2$ and $K_f = \sum\frac{1}{2}m v_f^2$.
• $K_f = K_i$: elastic (e.g., hard steel spheres, magnetic bumpers, atomic collisions).
• $K_f < K_i$, objects separate: inelastic; the missing kinetic energy becomes internal energy (thermal energy, sound, permanent deformation).
• $K_f < K_i$, objects move together: perfectly (completely) inelastic; this is the maximum possible kinetic energy loss consistent with momentum conservation.
• $K_f > K_i$: an internal energy source (spring, explosion) did positive work — an "explosion" or "release".
One-Dimensional Elastic Collision with a Target at Rest (useful results; on the exam, derive from $p$ and $K$ conservation): $$v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1, \qquad v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1$$ • $m_1 = m_2$: velocities exchange ($v_1' = 0$, $v_2' = v_1$).
• $m_1 \ll m_2$: the light object bounces back with nearly its original speed.
• $m_1 \gg m_2$: the heavy object continues almost unchanged; the light target leaves at nearly $2v_1$.
• Relative speed is preserved in 1D elastic collisions: $v_2' - v_1' = v_1 - v_2$.
Ballistic Pendulum — Two Stages, Two Laws:
• Stage 1 (bullet embeds in block): momentum conserved, kinetic energy not conserved: $mv = (m + M)V$.
• Stage 2 (block swings up): mechanical energy conserved: $\frac{1}{2}(m + M)V^2 = (m + M)gh$.
• Combined: $v = \frac{m + M}{m}\sqrt{2gh}$. Applying energy conservation across the collision is the most common error.
"Elastic" Trap: "The carts bounced apart, so the collision was elastic" is not a valid argument. Separation does not imply $K_f = K_i$. Always show the kinetic energy calculation, e.g. gliders of $1.0\text{ kg}$ each: $K_i = 8.0\text{ J}$, $K_f = 5.0\text{ J}$, so the collision is inelastic even though the gliders separate.

Units 1–4 treated every object as a point. Real objects have size, and a force applied off-centre can make them turn. Unit 5 builds a complete rotational copy of Newtonian mechanics: angle, angular velocity, and angular acceleration replace position, velocity, and acceleration; torque replaces force; rotational inertia replaces mass. Below are the definitions, models, and FRQ habits expected by the current AP Physics 1 CED (Unit 5, Topics 5.1–5.6). Rotational energy and angular momentum follow in Unit 6.

5.1 Rotational Kinematics CED Topic 5.1 • Angle, ω, α

Rigid-body model: every point of a rigid object rotating about a fixed axis turns through the same angle in the same time. We therefore describe the whole object with one set of angular quantities: $$\Delta\theta \;(\text{rad}), \qquad \omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt} \;(\text{rad/s}), \qquad \alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} \;(\text{rad/s}^2)$$ Choose a positive sense of rotation (usually counterclockwise) and keep the signs, exactly as in one-dimensional kinematics. One revolution is $2\pi\text{ rad} = 360^\circ$.

Constant Angular Acceleration — the Translational Analogues: $$\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad \Delta\theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2, \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\,\Delta\theta, \qquad \Delta\theta = \tfrac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}\,t$$ • Example: a fan spinning at $30\text{ rad/s}$ slows uniformly to rest in $6.0\text{ s}$. $\Delta\theta = \frac{30 + 0}{2}(6.0) = 90\text{ rad} = \frac{90}{2\pi} \approx 14\text{ rev}$.
• Graphs work exactly as in Unit 1: the slope of $\theta$–$t$ is $\omega$, the slope of $\omega$–$t$ is $\alpha$, and the area under $\omega$–$t$ is $\Delta\theta$.
Unit Trap: The kinematic equations need angles in radians. Convert revolutions with $1\text{ rev} = 2\pi\text{ rad}$ before substituting, and convert back only at the end. Multiplying by $2\pi$ when you should divide is one of the most common MCQ errors.
5.2 Connecting Linear and Rotational Motion CED Topic 5.2 • s = rθ, v = rω, a = rα

A point at distance $r$ from the axis travels along an arc. Its linear quantities follow from the angular ones (angles in radians): $$s = r\,\Delta\theta, \qquad v_t = r\omega, \qquad a_t = r\alpha, \qquad a_c = \frac{v_t^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$ All points share $\omega$ and $\alpha$, but points farther from the axis have larger tangential speed and larger accelerations.

Strings, Pulleys, and Drums (no slipping):
• A light string that unwinds from a drum of radius $R$ without slipping moves with $v = R\omega$ and $a = R\alpha$. This constraint equation links a hanging block to a rotating pulley.
• Example: a bucket descends with $a = 1.2\text{ m/s}^2$ on a rope wound around a drum of radius $0.20\text{ m}$. Then $\alpha = \frac{a}{R} = 6.0\text{ rad/s}^2$.
• Tangential acceleration $a_t = r\alpha$ changes the speed; centripetal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 r$ changes the direction. For a point on a speeding-up wheel, both are present and perpendicular.
"Same Speed" Trap: Two children on a merry-go-round at radii $r$ and $2r$ have the same angular velocity, but the outer child's tangential speed is twice as large. "Further out spins faster" is only true for $v$, never for $\omega$.
5.3 Torque CED Topic 5.3 • Lever Arm & Line of Action

Torque measures how effectively a force changes an object's rotation about a chosen axis: $$\tau = rF\sin\theta = r_\perp F = r F_\perp$$ Here $r$ is the distance from the axis to the point where the force is applied, and $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{F}$. The lever arm $r_\perp = r\sin\theta$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force. SI unit: $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ (not a joule).

Three Equivalent Pictures of the Same Torque:
• Perpendicular component of the force times the full distance: $\tau = rF_\perp$.
• Full force times the lever arm: $\tau = r_\perp F$.
• Example: a $40\text{ N}$ force on a $0.25\text{ m}$ wrench at $60^\circ$ to the handle gives $\tau = (0.25)(40)\sin 60^\circ \approx 8.7\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$.
• A force whose line of action passes through the axis (pushing a door toward its hinge) produces zero torque however large it is.
Sign Convention and Net Torque: assign counterclockwise (CCW) torques a positive sign and clockwise (CW) torques a negative sign, then add: $\tau_{\text{net}} = \sum \tau_i$. Example: $20\text{ N}$ tangent at $0.50\text{ m}$ (CCW) and $30\text{ N}$ tangent at $0.20\text{ m}$ (CW) give $\tau_{\text{net}} = 10 - 6.0 = +4.0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$, CCW, even though the clockwise force is larger.
Gravity Acts at the Center of Mass: for torque calculations, the weight of a uniform beam acts at its geometric centre. Placing $Mg$ at the end of the beam, or forgetting it entirely, costs points on almost every statics FRQ.
5.4 Rotational Inertia CED Topic 5.4 • I = Σmr² • Parallel Axis Theorem

Rotational inertia (moment of inertia) is an object's resistance to changes in its rotation. It depends on the total mass and on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis: $$I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \qquad (\text{point masses}), \qquad I = \int r^2\,dm \qquad (\text{continuous objects})$$ The same object has different rotational inertias about different axes. Mass far from the axis contributes much more, because $r$ is squared.

Standard Results (mass $M$, radius $R$, length $L$; provided or derivable):
• Thin hoop or thin-walled cylinder about its central axis: $I = MR^2$.
• Solid disk or solid cylinder about its central axis: $I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$.
• Solid sphere about a diameter: $I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$.
• Uniform thin rod about its centre: $I = \frac{1}{12}ML^2$; about one end: $I = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$.
• For equal $M$ and $R$: $I_{\text{hoop}} > I_{\text{disk}} > I_{\text{sphere}}$, because the hoop places all of its mass at the maximum distance.
Parallel Axis Theorem: if $I_{cm}$ is the rotational inertia about an axis through the center of mass, the rotational inertia about any parallel axis a distance $d$ away is $$I = I_{cm} + Md^2$$ • Rod about one end: $\frac{1}{12}ML^2 + M\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$. Check: $M = 3.0\text{ kg}$, $L = 1.2\text{ m}$ gives $0.36 + 1.08 = 1.44\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$.
• Solid disk about a point on its rim: $\frac{1}{2}MR^2 + MR^2 = \frac{3}{2}MR^2$.
• $I_{cm}$ is always the smallest rotational inertia among all parallel axes; $d$ is measured from the center of mass, not from an end.
Point-Mass Trap: for two $1.0\text{ kg}$ masses on the ends of a light $2.0\text{ m}$ rod, $I = 2.0\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ about the centre but $I = (1.0)(2.0)^2 + (1.0)(0)^2 = 4.0\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ about one end. A mass located on the axis contributes nothing.
5.5 Rotational Equilibrium and Newton's First Law in Rotational Form CED Topic 5.5 • Statics

Newton's first law in rotational form: if the net torque on a rigid object is zero, its angular velocity is constant (it stays at rest or keeps rotating at the same rate). An object in static equilibrium satisfies two independent conditions: $$\sum \vec{F} = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum \tau = 0 \;\text{ about any axis}$$ Because the net torque is zero about every axis when both conditions hold, you are free to choose the most convenient pivot.

Statics Algorithm (beams, see-saws, ladders, booms):
1. Draw a force diagram of the extended object, placing each force at its point of application (weight at the center of mass).
2. Choose the pivot at the location of an unknown force; that force then has zero lever arm and drops out of the torque equation.
3. Write $\sum\tau = 0$ with signs, then $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$ for the remaining unknowns.
• Example: a $4.0\text{ m}$, $20\text{ kg}$ uniform plank on two end supports with a $60\text{ kg}$ person $1.0\text{ m}$ from the left end ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Torques about the left end: $4.0F_R = (200)(2.0) + (600)(1.0) \Rightarrow F_R = 250\text{ N}$; then $F_L = 800 - 250 = 550\text{ N}$.
Hinged Boom with a Cable: for a horizontal uniform beam hinged at a wall and held by a cable at angle $\theta$ to the beam, take torques about the hinge so the unknown hinge force disappears: $T\sin\theta\,L = M g\frac{L}{2} + m_{\text{sign}} g L$. Only the component of the tension perpendicular to the beam produces torque.
Equilibrium ≠ Equal Forces: a see-saw balances when torques are equal, not when weights are equal: $m_1 g d_1 = m_2 g d_2$. A $30\text{ kg}$ child at $2.0\text{ m}$ balances a $40\text{ kg}$ child at $1.5\text{ m}$. Likewise, a ladder against a frictionless wall needs floor friction equal to the wall's normal force; the ladder's weight acts at its midpoint.
5.6 Newton's Second Law in Rotational Form CED Topic 5.6 • τ_net = Iα

Newton's second law for rotation about a fixed axis: $$\alpha = \frac{\tau_{\text{net}}}{I} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha$$ The angular acceleration is proportional to the net torque and inversely proportional to the rotational inertia. A large torque on a small-$I$ object gives a large $\alpha$.

Hanging Block on a Massive Pulley (pulley = solid disk, frictionless axle, string does not slip):
• Block (translation, $+$ downward): $mg - T = ma$.
• Pulley (rotation): $TR = I\alpha = \frac{1}{2}MR^2\alpha$.
• Constraint: $a = R\alpha$, so $T = \frac{1}{2}Ma$.
• Combine: $$a = \frac{mg}{m + \frac{1}{2}M}, \qquad T = \frac{\frac{1}{2}M\,mg}{m + \frac{1}{2}M} < mg$$ • Example ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$): $m = 2.0\text{ kg}$, $M = 4.0\text{ kg}$: $a = \frac{19.6}{2.0 + 2.0} = 4.9\text{ m/s}^2$.
• In a two-block Atwood machine with a massive pulley, the tensions on the two sides are different; their difference $(T_1 - T_2)R$ is the net torque that angularly accelerates the pulley. Only an ideal massless pulley has equal tensions.
Rod Pivoted at One End, Released from Horizontal: gravity acts at $L/2$, so $\tau = Mg\frac{L}{2}$ and $I = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$, giving $\alpha = \frac{3g}{2L}$. The tip's initial acceleration $a = \alpha L = \frac{3}{2}g$ is larger than $g$ — a favourite QQT result.
Tension = Weight Trap: if the hanging block accelerates downward, the string tension is less than $mg$. Substituting $T = mg$ into the pulley's torque equation ignores the block's own inertia and overestimates $\alpha$.

6.1 Rotational Kinetic Energy CED Topic 6.1 • K = ½Iω²

Every particle of a rotating rigid object moves with speed $v_i = r_i\omega$. Adding their kinetic energies gives $$K_{\text{rot}} = \sum \tfrac{1}{2} m_i (r_i\omega)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\sum m_i r_i^2\Big)\omega^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} I\omega^2$$ Rotational kinetic energy is not a new kind of energy: it is ordinary kinetic energy of the particles, organised by the axis. It is a scalar, measured in joules, and is always positive.

Worked Example — Spinning Sphere: a solid sphere ($I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$) of mass $2.0\text{ kg}$ and radius $0.10\text{ m}$ spins at $50\text{ rad/s}$ about a fixed diameter.
• $I = \frac{2}{5}(2.0)(0.10)^2 = 8.0\times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$.
• $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}(8.0\times 10^{-3})(50)^2 = 10\text{ J}$.
• For the same $M$, $R$, and $\omega$, a hoop ($\beta = 1$) stores $2.5$ times as much energy as the sphere ($\beta = \frac{2}{5}$), because $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}\beta MR^2\omega^2$ is proportional to $\beta$.
System Choice: a rotating object that is fixed to a stationary axle has only rotational kinetic energy. An object that both translates and rotates has $$K_{\text{total}} = \tfrac{1}{2}Mv_{cm}^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}I_{cm}\omega^2$$ This split is the foundation of rolling (Topic 6.5).
Squaring Trap: doubling $\omega$ quadruples $K_{\text{rot}}$. Writing $\frac{1}{2}I\omega$ (forgetting the square) gives an answer with the wrong units, $\text{J}\cdot\text{s}$, which is actually angular momentum divided by two.
6.2 Torque and Work CED Topic 6.2 • W = τΔθ • P = τω

A force that produces a torque about a fixed axis does work as the object turns. For a constant torque acting through an angular displacement $\Delta\theta$ (in radians): $$W = \tau\,\Delta\theta, \qquad \text{and for a varying torque} \qquad W = \int \tau\,d\theta = \text{area under the } \tau\text{–}\theta \text{ graph}$$ The rotational work–energy theorem then reads $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}I\omega_i^2$. The rate of doing work is the rotational power $P = \tau\omega$.

Worked Example — Grindstone: a constant torque of $5.0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ turns a grindstone through $20\text{ rad}$.
• $W = (5.0)(20) = 100\text{ J}$, so the grindstone gains $100\text{ J}$ of rotational kinetic energy (frictionless axle).
• If the angular displacement is given as $3.0$ revolutions, convert first: $\Delta\theta = 3.0 \times 2\pi \approx 18.8\text{ rad}$.
• A motor that supplies $8.0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ while turning at a steady $150\text{ rad/s}$ delivers $P = \tau\omega = 1200\text{ W}$.
Graphical Reading (Translation Between Representations): on a $\tau$–$\theta$ graph the signed area is work; area below the axis is negative work that reduces $K_{\text{rot}}$. This is the exact analogue of the $F$–$x$ graph in Topic 3.2.
N·m Is Not Always a Joule: torque and work share the unit $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, but torque is never "energy". Only the product of torque and an angle in radians is work. Using revolutions or degrees in $W = \tau\Delta\theta$ is the most common numerical error in this topic.
6.3 Angular Momentum and Angular Impulse CED Topic 6.3 • L = Iω • τΔt = ΔL

Angular momentum is the rotational analogue of linear momentum. Its magnitude depends on the chosen axis: $$L = I\omega \;\;(\text{rigid object about a fixed axis}), \qquad L = m v r_\perp = mvr\sin\theta \;\;(\text{point particle})$$ Here $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the particle's line of motion. A particle moving in a straight line still has angular momentum about any point that is not on that line. SI unit: $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

Angular Impulse — Newton's Second Law in Momentum Form: $$\tau_{\text{net}} = \frac{\Delta L}{\Delta t} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \text{angular impulse} = \tau_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t = \Delta L$$ • Example: a torque of $0.60\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ acts for $4.0\text{ s}$ on a wheel with $I = 0.30\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ that starts at rest. $\Delta L = (0.60)(4.0) = 2.4\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}$, so $\omega = \frac{2.4}{0.30} = 8.0\text{ rad/s}$.
• On an $L$–$t$ graph the slope is the net torque; on a $\tau$–$t$ graph the area is the change in angular momentum.
Straight-Line Trap: "the ball is not rotating, so $L = 0$" is false. A $0.50\text{ kg}$ ball moving at $4.0\text{ m/s}$ along a line that passes $3.0\text{ m}$ from point $P$ has $L = (0.50)(4.0)(3.0) = 6.0\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ about $P$, and this value stays constant while no torque acts about $P$.
6.4 Conservation of Angular Momentum CED Topic 6.4 • ΣL_i = ΣL_f when τ_ext = 0

If the net external torque on a system is zero, the total angular momentum of the system is constant. Internal torques (muscles, friction between two parts of the system, a child grabbing a merry-go-round) transfer angular momentum between parts but cannot change the total: $$\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; L_i = L_f \;\Longrightarrow\; I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f$$ Angular momentum may be conserved while kinetic energy is not, exactly as in an inelastic collision in Unit 4.

Worked Example — Rotating Stool: a student on a frictionless stool has $I = 3.0\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ at $2.0\text{ rad/s}$, then pulls two dumbbells inward so that $I = 1.8\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$.
• $\omega_f = \frac{(3.0)(2.0)}{1.8} \approx 3.3\text{ rad/s}$.
• $K_i = \frac{1}{2}(3.0)(2.0)^2 = 6.0\text{ J}$; $K_f = \frac{L^2}{2I_f} = \frac{(6.0)^2}{2(1.8)} = 10\text{ J}$. The extra $4.0\text{ J}$ comes from the work done by the student's arms (internal chemical energy).
Rotational "Collisions":
• A disk ($I_A$, $\omega_0$) and a non-rotating coaxial disk ($I_B$) stick together: $\omega_f = \frac{I_A}{I_A + I_B}\omega_0$, and the fraction of kinetic energy kept is $\frac{I_A}{I_A + I_B}$.
• A child running tangentially onto the rim of a merry-go-round: $m v R = \left(I_{\text{disk}} + mR^2\right)\omega_f$. The child's linear motion carries angular momentum $mvR$ about the axle.
Wrong Conserved Quantity: when a skater pulls her arms in, $L$ is constant but $K_{\text{rot}}$ increases. Setting $\frac{1}{2}I_i\omega_i^2 = \frac{1}{2}I_f\omega_f^2$ gives the wrong final speed. Conversely, never assume $\omega$ is unchanged because "the mass did not change".
6.5 Rolling CED Topic 6.5 • v_cm = Rω • Energy Sharing

Rolling without slipping: the point of the object in contact with the surface is instantaneously at rest relative to the surface. This gives the constraint $$v_{cm} = R\omega, \qquad a_{cm} = R\alpha$$ Relative to the ground, the contact point has speed $0$, the centre moves at $v_{cm}$, and the top of the wheel moves at $2v_{cm}$. Because the contact point does not slide, static friction does no work on an object that rolls without slipping, so mechanical energy is conserved.

Energy Sharing for $I = \beta MR^2$: $$K_{\text{total}} = \tfrac{1}{2}Mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta MR^2\left(\tfrac{v}{R}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(1+\beta)Mv^2, \qquad \frac{K_{\text{rot}}}{K_{\text{total}}} = \frac{\beta}{1+\beta}$$ • Hoop ($\beta = 1$): $\frac{1}{2}$ of the kinetic energy is rotational.
• Solid disk or cylinder ($\beta = \frac{1}{2}$): $\frac{1}{3}$ rotational, $\frac{2}{3}$ translational.
• Solid sphere ($\beta = \frac{2}{5}$): $\frac{2}{7}$ rotational, $\frac{5}{7}$ translational.
Rolling Down an Incline from Rest (height $h$): $Mgh = \frac{1}{2}(1+\beta)Mv^2$, so $$v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+\beta}}$$ • Example: a solid disk rolls down from $h = 1.2\text{ m}$ ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$): $v = \sqrt{\frac{4gh}{3}} \approx 4.0\text{ m/s}$, smaller than the $4.8\text{ m/s}$ of a frictionless sliding block.
• The result is independent of $M$ and $R$. In a race, the object with the smallest $\beta$ wins: sphere, then disk, then hoop.
Slipping Versus Rolling: if a ball slides and spins (bowling ball just after release), $v_{cm} \neq R\omega$, kinetic friction acts at the contact point and mechanical energy is dissipated. Use $v = R\omega$ and energy conservation only after the problem states "rolls without slipping".
6.6 Motion of Orbiting Satellites CED Topic 6.6 • Energy & L in Orbits

For a satellite of mass $m$ orbiting a much more massive central object $M$, gravity always points toward the centre of $M$, so it exerts zero torque about that centre. In the satellite–planet system, both the satellite's angular momentum about the planet and the total mechanical energy are conserved: $$L = m v r_\perp = \text{constant}, \qquad E = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = \text{constant}$$

Circular Orbits (radius $r$): gravity supplies the centripetal force, $\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$, so $$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad K = \frac{GMm}{2r}, \qquad U_G = -\frac{GMm}{r}, \qquad E = -\frac{GMm}{2r} = -K$$ • A larger orbit has a smaller speed and smaller $K$, but a larger (less negative) total energy, so raising a satellite requires positive work $\Delta E = \frac{GMm}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)$.
Elliptical Orbits and Escape:
• At the closest and farthest points $\vec{v} \perp \vec{r}$, so $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$. Example ($GM = 4.0\times 10^{14}\text{ m}^3/\text{s}^2$, $r_p = 7.0\times 10^{6}\text{ m}$, $r_a = 1.4\times 10^{7}\text{ m}$): $v_p \approx 8.73\text{ km/s}$ and $v_a = \frac{r_p}{r_a}v_p \approx 4.36\text{ km/s}$; both points give the same specific energy $\frac{E}{m} \approx -1.9\times 10^{7}\text{ J/kg}$.
• Escape speed is the launch speed for which $E = 0$: $\frac{1}{2}mv_{esc}^2 - \frac{GMm}{R} = 0 \Rightarrow v_{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}$. For Earth ($M = 6.0\times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$, $R = 6.4\times 10^{6}\text{ m}$), $v_{esc} \approx 11.2\text{ km/s}$.
Sign of Orbital Energy: a bound orbit has $E < 0$. Writing $E = K + |U|$, or using $U = mgh$ for distances comparable to the planet's radius, gives energies that are wrong in both sign and size.

Qualitative/Quantitative Translation (QQT) — Rubric Notes:
• A typical prompt: "A hoop and a solid disk of equal mass and radius roll from rest down the same incline. Without equations, explain which reaches the bottom first. Then derive $v$ for each and show that your derivation agrees with your explanation."
• Qualitative point: the disk has less of its mass far from the axis, so a smaller share of $Mgh$ goes into rotation and more into translation; the disk is faster.
• Quantitative point: $v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+\beta}}$ with $\beta_{\text{disk}} = \frac{1}{2} < \beta_{\text{hoop}} = 1$.
• Consistency point: you must explicitly connect the two ("the smaller $\beta$ in the denominator gives a larger $v$, as argued in part (a)"). Graders award this point separately.
Experimental Design and Analysis — Measuring $\beta$ by Rolling:
1. Release the object from rest at several measured heights $h$ (metre stick) and measure the speed at the bottom with a photogate (object diameter divided by blocking time).
2. Repeat each height several times and average to reduce random error.
3. Linearize: $v^2 = \frac{2g}{1+\beta}\,h$. Plot $v^2$ on the vertical axis against $h$; the best-fit line should pass near the origin.
4. From the slope $k$: $\beta = \frac{2g}{k} - 1$. Example: a slope of $14.0\text{ m/s}^2$ with $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ gives $\beta = 0.40$, consistent with a solid sphere.
5. Name a real source of error: slipping at steep angles or rolling friction reduces $v$ and makes $\beta$ appear too large.
Habits Across the Four Current FRQ Types:
• Mathematical Routines: state the conserved quantity and the reason ("no external torque about the axle, so $L$ is conserved") before writing the equation.
• Translation Between Representations: draw energy bar charts with separate bars for $K_{\text{trans}}$, $K_{\text{rot}}$, and $U_g$; for a rolling disk the final $K_{\text{rot}}$ bar is exactly half the $K_{\text{trans}}$ bar.
• Experimental Design and Analysis: always say which quantity goes on each axis and how the slope gives the target quantity.
• QQT: justify every "increases/decreases/stays the same" with a named principle (conservation of $L$, conservation of $E$, rotational work–energy theorem).
Five Frequent Point-Losers:
1. Conserving kinetic energy in a rotational collision or when a skater pulls her arms in; only $L$ is conserved.
2. Forgetting $K_{\text{rot}}$ for a rolling object and writing $v = \sqrt{2gh}$.
3. Using revolutions instead of radians in $W = \tau\Delta\theta$ or $L = I\omega$.
4. Claiming that static friction removes energy from an object that rolls without slipping.
5. Treating orbital total energy as positive, or using $mgh$ for satellite altitude changes.

AP Physics 1 — Unit 7: Oscillations — Simple Harmonic Motion, Period, Graphs, and Energy

Units 2 and 3 described motion caused by forces and tracked with energy. Unit 7 applies both tools to one special and very common kind of motion: an object that moves back and forth about a stable equilibrium position. The current AP Physics 1 CED (Unit 7, Topics 7.1–7.4; exam weighting 5–8%) covers the definition of simple harmonic motion (SHM), the period and frequency of the spring–object oscillator and the simple pendulum, graphical and equation representations of SHM, and the energy of simple harmonic oscillators. Hooke's law ($F_s = -kx$) and elastic potential energy ($U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$) are taught in Unit 3; here they are used, not re-derived.

7.1 Defining Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) CED Topic 7.1 • F = −kx • Restoring Force

Periodic motion repeats itself after a fixed time interval. Simple harmonic motion is a special case of periodic motion. Two definitions are needed:
• An equilibrium position is a location at which the net force exerted on the object (or system) is zero.
• A restoring force is a force exerted in the direction opposite to the object's displacement from that equilibrium position.

SHM results when the magnitude of the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium: $$F_{\text{net}} = -kx \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad a = -\frac{k}{m}x$$ The minus sign carries the physics: the force (and therefore the acceleration) always points back toward equilibrium, and it grows linearly as the object moves farther away.

Worked Example — Reading the Restoring Force: a block on a horizontal spring with $k = 200\text{ N/m}$ is held $0.15\text{ m}$ to the right of equilibrium (the $+x$ direction).
• $F_x = -kx = -(200)(0.15) = -30\text{ N}$: a $30\text{ N}$ force directed to the left, toward equilibrium.
• At $x = -0.15\text{ m}$ the force is $+30\text{ N}$; at $x = 0$ it is zero. A graph of $F$ versus $x$ is a straight line through the origin with slope $-k$.
The Vertical Spring and the Pendulum:
• A block of mass $m$ hanging from a spring has its equilibrium position where the spring is stretched by $\Delta y_0 = \frac{mg}{k}$, not at the spring's natural length. Measured from this new equilibrium, the net force is still $-ky$, so the motion is SHM with the same $k$.
• For a simple pendulum, the gravitational force exerts a restoring torque about the pivot of magnitude $mgL\sin\theta$. For small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ (in radians), so the restoring torque is approximately proportional to the angular displacement and the motion can be modelled as SHM. At $10^\circ$, $\sin\theta = 0.1736$ while $\theta = 0.1745\text{ rad}$: an error of only about $0.5\%$.
"Restoring" Is Not Enough: a force that always points toward equilibrium but has constant magnitude (a ball bouncing between two ramps), or grows like $x^3$, produces periodic motion that is not simple harmonic. The CED test is proportionality: doubling $x$ must double $|F_{\text{net}}|$.
7.2 Frequency and Period of SHM CED Topic 7.2 • T = 1/f • T = 2π√(m/k) • T = 2π√(L/g)

The period $T$ is the time for one complete cycle; the frequency $f$ is the number of cycles per second (hertz). They are reciprocals: $$T = \frac{1}{f}$$ For the two standard oscillators on the AP Physics 1 equation sheet: $$T_{s} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \;\;(\text{object–ideal-spring}), \qquad T_{p} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \;\;(\text{simple pendulum, small angle})$$

Worked Examples:
• Spring: $m = 0.50\text{ kg}$, $k = 200\text{ N/m}$ gives $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.0025} = 2\pi(0.050) \approx 0.31\text{ s}$ and $f = \frac{1}{T} \approx 3.2\text{ Hz}$.
• Pendulum: $L = 1.0\text{ m}$, $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ gives $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1.0}{9.8}} \approx 2.0\text{ s}$. A pendulum that ticks once per second each way (a "seconds pendulum", $T = 2.0\text{ s}$) needs $L = \frac{g}{\pi^2} \approx 0.99\text{ m}$.
Functional Dependence (Skill 2.D):
• Spring: $T \propto \sqrt{m}$ and $T \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$. Quadrupling the mass doubles the period; quadrupling $k$ halves it. The spring period does not depend on $g$, so a vertical spring has the same period as a horizontal one, on Earth or on the Moon.
• Pendulum: $T \propto \sqrt{L}$ and $T \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}$. The pendulum period does not depend on the bob's mass, because both the restoring force and the inertia are proportional to $m$.
• Neither period depends on the amplitude (as long as the pendulum angle stays small).
Experimental Design — Measuring $g$ or $k$ (Skill 3.A): time $10$ or $20$ complete oscillations and divide, so that your reaction-time error is shared over many cycles. Linearize before graphing: plot $T^2$ against $L$ (slope $\frac{4\pi^2}{g}$) or $T^2$ against $m$ (slope $\frac{4\pi^2}{k}$). For example, a $T^2$–$L$ slope of $4.03\text{ s}^2/\text{m}$ gives $g = \frac{4\pi^2}{4.03} \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.
Ratio Inversion and the Missing Square Root: writing $2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ or $2\pi\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}$ gives a quantity with units of $\text{s}^{-1}$, not seconds. Check units before you trust the number. Likewise, a factor-of-4 change in $m$ or $L$ changes $T$ by a factor of 2, not 4.
7.3 Representing and Analyzing SHM CED Topic 7.3 • x = A cos(2πft) • Extrema & Zeros

The displacement of an object in SHM, measured from equilibrium, is a sinusoidal function of time: $$x = A\cos(2\pi f t) \quad \text{(released from rest at } x = A \text{ when } t = 0\text{)}, \qquad x = A\sin(2\pi f t) \quad \text{(passing } x = 0 \text{ when } t = 0\text{)}$$ $A$ is the amplitude, the maximum displacement from equilibrium (half of the total peak-to-peak distance). The angle $2\pi f t$ is in radians.

• Velocity is a quarter cycle "out of step" with displacement: when $x = A\cos(2\pi ft)$ starts at $+A$, the velocity starts at zero and reaches its most negative value at $t = \frac{T}{4}$, as the object passes equilibrium moving in the $-x$ direction.
• Acceleration is always opposite to displacement ($a = -\frac{k}{m}x$), so the $a$–$t$ graph is the $x$–$t$ graph flipped upside down and scaled.
Worked Example — Reading an Equation: $x = (0.12\text{ m})\cos(4\pi t)$ with $t$ in seconds.
• Compare with $A\cos(2\pi f t)$: $A = 0.12\text{ m}$, $2\pi f = 4\pi \Rightarrow f = 2.0\text{ Hz}$, $T = 0.50\text{ s}$.
• At $t = 0.125\text{ s} = \frac{T}{4}$: $x = 0.12\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$, speed maximum, acceleration zero. At $t = 0.25\text{ s} = \frac{T}{2}$: $x = -0.12\text{ m}$, momentarily at rest, acceleration maximum in the $+x$ direction.
• The maximum acceleration magnitude follows from Newton's second law at a turning point: $a_{\max} = \frac{kA}{m}$.
Amplitude Does Not Change the Period: pulling a spring–block system twice as far makes the block travel twice the distance, but the larger restoring force makes it move proportionally faster, so $T$ is unchanged (Essential Knowledge 7.3.A.2). Also avoid reading the peak-to-peak distance on a graph as the amplitude.
7.4 Energy of Simple Harmonic Oscillators CED Topic 7.4 • E = K + U = ½kA²

The total mechanical energy of a system in SHM is the sum of its kinetic and potential energies, and with no dissipative forces it is constant: $$E_{\text{total}} = K + U = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$$ At the turning points $K = 0$ and $U$ is maximum; at equilibrium $U$ is minimum and $K$ is maximum. The minimum kinetic energy is zero. Because $E_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$, changing the amplitude changes the total energy: doubling $A$ quadruples $E$.

Worked Example — Spring–Block Energy Table: $k = 50\text{ N/m}$, $m = 0.50\text{ kg}$, $A = 0.20\text{ m}$.
• $E = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.20)^2 = 1.0\text{ J}$.
• At $x = 0$: $K = 1.0\text{ J}$, so $v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.0)}{0.50}} = 2.0\text{ m/s}$. Equivalently, $v_{\max} = A\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$.
• At $x = 0.10\text{ m} = \frac{A}{2}$: $U = \frac{1}{2}(50)(0.10)^2 = 0.25\text{ J}$, so $K = 0.75\text{ J}$ and $v = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.7\text{ m/s}$. Half the amplitude stores only one quarter of the energy as potential energy.
• Kinetic and potential energy are equal where $\frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}kA^2\right)$, that is at $x = \pm\frac{A}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \pm 0.71A$.
Pendulum Energy: for a pendulum the potential energy is gravitational, $U_g = mgL(1 - \cos\theta)$ measured from the lowest point. A $1.0\text{ m}$ pendulum released from rest at $10^\circ$ ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$) rises $L(1-\cos 10^\circ) \approx 0.015\text{ m}$ above its lowest point, so $v_{\max} = \sqrt{2gL(1-\cos\theta_0)} \approx 0.55\text{ m/s}$.
Changing the System Mid-Oscillation (CED Sample Activity): a blob of clay of equal mass is dropped vertically onto a cart oscillating on a horizontal spring and sticks at a turning point, where the cart is momentarily at rest. No kinetic energy is lost and the spring's stretch is unchanged, so $E$ and $A$ stay the same; the mass doubles, so $T$ grows by $\sqrt{2}$ and $v_{\max} = A\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ falls by $\sqrt{2}$. If the clay lands as the cart passes equilibrium instead, the collision is perfectly inelastic, kinetic energy is lost, and the amplitude decreases.
Energy Is Quadratic, Not Linear: at $x = \frac{A}{2}$ the object does not have half its energy as potential energy, and its speed is not half of $v_{\max}$. Always use $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ and subtract from the total.

Experimental Design and Analysis — Measuring $k$ Two Ways (CED Sample Activity):
1. Static method: hang known masses, measure each stretch $\Delta y$ with a metre stick, plot $mg$ (vertical) against $\Delta y$ (horizontal); the slope is $k$.
2. Dynamic method: for each known mass, time $10$ oscillations with a stopwatch and compute $T$. Plot $T^2$ (vertical) against $m$ (horizontal); the slope is $\frac{4\pi^2}{k}$, so $k = \frac{4\pi^2}{\text{slope}}$.
3. Name a real source of error: the spring's own mass adds to the oscillating inertia, so the $T^2$–$m$ line has a small positive intercept; the slope is still valid.
Translation Between Representations (TBR):
• Sketch $x$–$t$, $v$–$t$, and $a$–$t$ on aligned time axes. Each zero of $x$ must line up with an extremum of $v$, and each extremum of $x$ with a zero of $v$ and an opposite-sign extremum of $a$.
• Energy bar charts: at a turning point draw only a $U$ bar; at equilibrium only a $K$ bar of equal height; at $x = \frac{A}{2}$ draw $U$ at one quarter and $K$ at three quarters of the total.
Qualitative/Quantitative Translation (QQT): a typical prompt asks what happens to the period when the bob of a pendulum is replaced by a heavier one, first in words, then by derivation. Qualitative: the gravitational restoring force and the inertia both scale with $m$, so their effects cancel. Quantitative: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$ contains no $m$. Consistency: state explicitly that the equation confirms the reasoning.
Five Frequent Point-Losers:
1. Claiming that a larger amplitude gives a longer period.
2. Saying the acceleration is zero at the turning points because the object is momentarily at rest.
3. Using degrees inside $\cos(2\pi f t)$ or inverting the ratio inside the square root.
4. Treating energy as linear in $x$ (for example, $U = \frac{E}{2}$ at $x = \frac{A}{2}$).
5. Placing the equilibrium of a hanging spring at its natural length instead of at a stretch of $\frac{mg}{k}$.
